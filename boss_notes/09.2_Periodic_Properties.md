# Periodic Properties
> **Topic:** Periodic Table | **Priority:** 🟡 MEDIUM | **Exam Frequency:** 2/7 exams

## 📌 Quick Summary
> This section details the major periodic properties (Atomic Radius, Ionization Potential, Electron Affinity, Electronegativity, and Metallic Character). It explains how and why these properties show regular, predictable gradation across periods and down groups based on the constant battle between effective nuclear charge and atomic shielding.

## 📖 Core Theory

### What are Periodic Properties?
Periodic properties are specific physical or chemical characteristics of elements that are directly tied to their electronic configuration. Because electronic configurations repeat periodically, these properties show a regular, predictable gradation as we move across a period (left to right) or down a group (top to bottom).

### 1. Atomic Radius (Size)
*   **Definition:** The distance from the center of the nucleus to the outermost shell of electrons.
*   **Across a Period (Decreases $\downarrow$):** As we move left to right, electrons are added to the *same* valence shell, but protons are simultaneously added to the nucleus. This increases the "effective nuclear charge," pulling the electron cloud closer to the nucleus and shrinking the atom.
*   **Down a Group (Increases $\uparrow$):** Entirely new principal electron shells are added. This increases the physical distance from the nucleus. Additionally, the inner electron shells "shield" the valence electrons from the nucleus, a factor which dominates the increased nuclear charge.

### 2. Ionization Potential (Energy)
*(Note: Covered in extreme depth in [09.3 Ionization Potential](09.3_Ionization_Potential.md))*
*   **Definition:** The minimum energy required to completely remove the most loosely bound electron from an isolated gaseous atom.
*   **Across a Period (Increases $\uparrow$):** Smaller atomic radius and higher effective nuclear charge mean electrons are held much tighter.
*   **Down a Group (Decreases $\downarrow$):** Larger atomic radius and higher shielding effect mean valence electrons are loosely held and easily removed.

### 3. Electron Affinity ($EA$)
*   **Definition:** It is a measurable, absolute energy value. It is defined as the exact amount of energy *released* when a single electron is added to the outermost shell of an isolated, neutral gaseous atom to form a stable negative ion (anion). It measures the raw attraction an isolated atom has for an extra electron.
*   **Across a Period (Increases / Becomes more negative $\uparrow$):** Non-metals on the right are closer to achieving a stable octet and have high effective nuclear charges, meaning they readily and violently accept electrons (releasing lots of energy). Halogens have the highest negative electron affinities.
*   **Down a Group (Decreases $\downarrow$):** The incoming electron goes into a higher, more distant energy level, feeling less pull from the nucleus.

### 4. Electronegativity ($EN$)
*   **Definition:** It is a relative, dimensionless scale (like the Pauling scale). It is defined as the relative tendency or ability of an atom, *when it is covalently bonded within a molecule*, to attract the shared pair of electrons firmly towards its own nucleus. It measures attraction in a *bonded state*.
*   **Across a Period (Increases $\uparrow$):** Smaller atoms with higher nuclear charge pull shared pairs much closer. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (4.0).
*   **Down a Group (Decreases $\downarrow$):** Larger atoms with more inner-shell shielding exert far less pull on outer shared pairs.

### 5. Metallic / Non-Metallic Character
*   **Across a Period (Metallic decreases, Non-metallic increases):** Elements transition from losing electrons easily (metals on the far left) to gaining them easily (non-metals on the far right).
*   **Down a Group (Metallic increases $\uparrow$):** Because Ionization Potential decreases as atoms get larger, it becomes easier to lose electrons. The ability to easily lose electrons is the fundamental definition of metallic character.

## 📊 Comparison Tables

### Electron Affinity ($EA$) vs. Electronegativity ($EN$)
| Feature | Electron Affinity ($EA$) | Electronegativity ($EN$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **State of Atom** | Measured for an **isolated, gaseous** atom. | Measured for an atom **bonded within a molecule**. |
| **Action** | The addition of a complete, discrete electron to form an anion. | The pulling of a *shared* pair of covalent electrons. |
| **Measurement** | An absolute, measurable quantity of energy (e.g., $kJ/mol$). | A relative, dimensionless number (e.g., Pauling scale). |
| **Highest Value** | Chlorine has the highest electron affinity. | Fluorine has the highest electronegativity. |

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes
*   **The EA vs. EN Trick:** Examiners love asking students to distinguish between these two because they sound identical. The golden rule: **Electron Affinity** is for an *isolated* atom taking a *whole* electron (measured in absolute energy). **Electronegativity** is for a *bonded* atom playing tug-of-war with *shared* electrons (measured on a relative, numberless scale). Mentioning "isolated" vs "bonded" is the key to full marks.
*   **The Size vs Charge Battle:** If you are ever stuck trying to explain *why* a property increases or decreases, default to the battle between **Effective Nuclear Charge** (which wins across a period) and **Atomic Size / Shielding Effect** (which wins down a group).
