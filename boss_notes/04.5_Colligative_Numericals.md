# Abnormal Colligative Properties
> **Topic:** Colligative Properties | **Priority:** 🟠 HIGH | **Exam Frequency:** 1/7 exams

## 📌 Quick Summary
> Colligative numericals in this course almost exclusively test the van 't Hoff equation ($\pi = CRT$) for osmotic pressure. The absolute most repeated question across four separate years asks for the osmotic pressure of a $5\%$ glucose solution. Abnormal colligative properties occur when a solute dissociates or associates in solution, requiring the van 't Hoff factor ($i$).

## 📖 Core Theory

### Abnormal Colligative Properties
Colligative properties depend strictly on the **number** of solute particles. The basic formulas ($\Delta T_b, \Delta T_f, \pi$) assume the solute is a non-electrolyte that neither associates nor dissociates in solution.
*   **Dissociation:** When an electrolyte like $NaCl$ dissolves, it splits into $Na^+$ and $Cl^-$. The number of particles doubles, making the observed colligative property (like osmotic pressure) roughly twice the expected normal value.
*   **Association:** When acetic acid dissolves in benzene, two molecules hydrogen-bond to form a single dimer. The number of particles halves, making the observed colligative property half the expected normal value.
*   **van 't Hoff factor ($i$):** A correction factor introduced to account for this abnormality.
    $$i = \frac{\text{Observed Colligative Property}}{\text{Calculated (Normal) Colligative Property}}$$
    *   $i > 1$: Dissociation is occurring.
    *   $i < 1$: Association is occurring.
    *   $i = 1$: Normal behavior (non-electrolytes like glucose).

## 🔢 Solved Numericals (The Golden Question)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The following numerical has appeared almost verbatim in **four different final exams**. Mastering the conversion of "5%" to Molarity is critical.

### 🎯 The "5% Glucose" Mega-Numerical (Appeared in 2023, 2021, 2020, 2019)
**Question:** Calculate the osmotic pressure of a 5% solution of glucose at $18^\circ C$ (or $20^\circ C$).

**Given Data:**
*   **Concentration:** "5% solution" implicitly means $5\% \text{ (w/v)}$. This translates to $5 \text{ grams}$ of solute dissolved in exactly $100 \text{ mL}$ ($0.1 \text{ Liters}$) of solution.
*   **Mass of solute ($W$):** $5 \text{ g}$
*   **Volume of solution ($V$):** $0.1 \text{ L}$
*   **Molar mass of glucose ($C_6H_{12}O_6$):** $(12 \times 6) + (1 \times 12) + (16 \times 6) = 180 \text{ g/mol}$
*   **Gas constant ($R$):** $0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}$

**Step-by-Step Solution:**

**Step 1: Calculate the Molar Concentration ($C$)**
First, find the number of moles of glucose ($n$):
$$n = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Molar Mass}} = \frac{5}{180} = 0.02778 \text{ moles}$$
Next, calculate Molarity ($C$):
$$C = \frac{n}{V} = \frac{0.02778 \text{ mol}}{0.1 \text{ L}} = 0.2778 \text{ mol/L}$$

**Step 2: Apply the van 't Hoff equation**
$$\pi = CRT$$
*(Note: Because glucose is a non-electrolyte, it does not dissociate, so the van 't Hoff factor $i = 1$).*

**Case A: If the temperature is $18^\circ C$ (from 2023, 2020, 2019 exams)**
*   $T = 18^\circ C = 18 + 273.15 = 291.15 \text{ K}$
*   $\pi = (0.2778 \text{ mol/L}) \times (0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}) \times (291.15 \text{ K})$
*   $\pi = 0.2778 \times 23.903$
*   **$\pi = 6.64 \text{ atmospheres}$**

**Case B: If the temperature is $20^\circ C$ (from 2021 exam)**
*   $T = 20^\circ C = 20 + 273.15 = 293.15 \text{ K}$
*   $\pi = (0.2778 \text{ mol/L}) \times (0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}) \times (293.15 \text{ K})$
*   $\pi = 0.2778 \times 24.067$
*   **$\pi = 6.68 \text{ atmospheres}$**

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes
*   **Assuming 5% means 5 moles:** The most common mistake is plugging $5$ directly into the concentration or mole variables. $5\%$ (w/v) strictly means $5 \text{ grams}$ per $100 \text{ mL}$. You must execute the mass-to-mole conversion ($5/180$).
*   **Volume Units in $\pi = CRT$:** The universal gas constant $R = 0.0821$ uses Liters. Therefore, the volume used to calculate Molarity ($C$) MUST be in Liters ($0.1 \text{ L}$), not milliliters ($100 \text{ mL}$).
*   **Temperature Conversion:** Never forget to add $273.15$ to the Celsius temperature to get Kelvin before multiplying.
