# pH and Ionic Equilibrium
> **Topic:** Acid-Base & Buffer | **Priority:** 🟡 MEDIUM | **Exam Frequency:** 0/7 exams

## 📌 Quick Summary
> This section covers the fundamental mathematics of aqueous solutions. It introduces the Ionic Product of Water ($K_w$), the definition of the pH and pOH scales, and the vital relationship between them ($pH + pOH = 14$). These concepts are the mandatory prerequisites for understanding Buffer Action.

## 📖 Core Theory

### 1. Auto-ionization of Water & Ionic Product ($K_w$)
Water is an extremely weak electrolyte and undergoes self-ionization (auto-ionization) to a very small extent:
> $H_2O(l) + H_2O(l) \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+(aq) + OH^-(aq)$
> *(Simplified as: $H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + OH^-$)*

Applying the Law of Mass Action, the equilibrium constant for this specific reaction is called the **Ionic Product of Water ($K_w$)**:
> **$$K_w = [H^+][OH^-]$$**

At standard room temperature ($25^\circ C$ or $298 K$), the value of $K_w$ is strictly constant:
> **$$K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \text{ mol}^2/\text{L}^2$$**
*(Since pure water is completely neutral, $[H^+] = [OH^-] = \sqrt{10^{-14}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-7} \text{ M}$).*

### 2. The pH and pOH Scales
Because hydrogen ion concentrations are often infinitesimally small numbers (e.g., $10^{-7} \text{ M}$), S.P.L. Sørensen introduced the logarithmic "p" scale to convert them into manageable integers.
*   **pH:** The negative base-10 logarithm of the active hydrogen ion concentration (in mol/L).
    > **$$pH = -\log_{10}[H^+]$$**
*   **pOH:** The negative base-10 logarithm of the active hydroxyl ion concentration.
    > **$$pOH = -\log_{10}[OH^-]$$**

### 3. The Relationship Between pH and pOH
We can derive the relationship by taking the negative logarithm of both sides of the $K_w$ equation:
$$K_w = [H^+][OH^-]$$
$$-\log(K_w) = -\log([H^+][OH^-])$$
$$pK_w = (-\log[H^+]) + (-\log[OH^-])$$
> **$$pK_w = pH + pOH$$**

Since $K_w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ at $25^\circ C$, then $pK_w = 14$. Therefore, at standard temperature:
> **$$pH + pOH = 14$$**

## 🔢 Solved Numericals

*(No direct past exam questions found, but standard calculation workflow is below).*

**Example: Calculate the pH of a $0.005 \text{ M}$ solution of $H_2SO_4$, assuming complete dissociation.**
1.  **Reaction:** $H_2SO_4 \rightarrow 2H^+ + SO_4^{2-}$
2.  **Concentration:** Since 1 mole of $H_2SO_4$ yields 2 moles of $H^+$, $[H^+] = 2 \times 0.005 = 0.01 \text{ M} = 10^{-2} \text{ M}$.
3.  **Formula:** $pH = -\log[H^+]$
4.  **Calculation:** $pH = -\log(10^{-2}) = 2$.
5.  **Answer:** The pH is 2.

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes
*   **The Temperature Trap:** Examiners sometimes try to trick students by asking about the pH of neutral water at high temperatures. Remember that auto-ionization is an *endothermic* process. If temperature rises (e.g., to $100^\circ C$), Le Chatelier's Principle states that the forward reaction is favored, so $K_w$ *increases* (e.g., to $\approx 10^{-12}$). This drops the $pK_w$ to 12. Therefore, neutral water at $100^\circ C$ has a pH of ~6.0, **not 7.0!** However, it is *still neutral* because $[H^+]$ still perfectly equals $[OH^-]$.
*   **Strong vs Weak Acids:** The formula $pH = -\log[\text{Acid}]$ only works for *strong* acids that dissociate 100%. For weak acids, you must use the acid dissociation constant ($K_a$) to find the actual $[H^+]$ before calculating pH.
