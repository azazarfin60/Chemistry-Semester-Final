# Enthalpy Derivation
> **Topic:** Thermochemistry | **Priority:** 🟠 HIGH | **Exam Frequency:** ?/7 exams

## 📌 Quick Summary
> The heat of a reaction depends fundamentally on the physical conditions under which it is carried out. This sub-topic proves mathematically that heat measured at constant volume ($q_v$) is exactly equal to the change in internal energy ($\Delta E$), while heat measured at constant pressure ($q_p$) is exactly equal to the change in enthalpy ($\Delta H$).

## 📖 Core Theory

### 1. Thermodynamic Conditions of Reactions
Most chemical reactions occur under one of two specific physical conditions:
1.  **Constant Volume ($q_v$):** Reactions happening in a sealed, completely rigid container (like a bomb calorimeter). Because the volume cannot change, no expansion or contraction work can be done by the gases.
2.  **Constant Pressure ($q_p$):** Reactions happening in open vessels (like beakers) exposed to a constant atmospheric pressure. Here, the system can expand or contract, meaning it performs pressure-volume work ($P\Delta V$).

Because the vast majority of real-world chemistry occurs in open systems at constant pressure, a specific thermodynamic state function called **Enthalpy ($H$)** was introduced. Enthalpy is defined mathematically as the sum of a system's internal energy and its pressure-volume energy:
> $H = E + PV$

### 2. Exothermic vs. Endothermic Reactions
*   **Exothermic Reaction:** A chemical reaction accompanied by the **release or evolution of heat** energy into the surroundings. Because heat is lost, the total enthalpy of the products is less than the reactants, resulting in a **negative $\Delta H$**. The temperature of the reaction mixture rises.
    *   *Example:* Combustion of Methane. $CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(l); \quad \Delta H = -890.3 \text{ kJ/mol}$
*   **Endothermic Reaction:** A chemical reaction accompanied by the **absorption of heat** energy from the surroundings. The enthalpy of the products is greater than the reactants, resulting in a **positive $\Delta H$**. The temperature of the reaction mixture drops.
    *   *Example:* Thermal decomposition of Calcium Carbonate. $CaCO_3(s) \rightarrow CaO(s) + CO_2(g); \quad \Delta H = +178 \text{ kJ/mol}$

## 🧮 Key Derivations & Equations

### Derivation 1: Heat of Reaction at Constant Volume ($q_v = \Delta E$)
1.  According to the First Law of Thermodynamics, the heat ($q$) absorbed by a system is used to increase its internal energy ($\Delta E$) and to do work of expansion ($W$).
    $$q = \Delta E + W$$
2.  The work done by a gas expanding or contracting is given by $W = P\Delta V$.
3.  If the reaction is carried out in a completely closed vessel at a constant volume, the change in volume ($\Delta V$) is exactly zero. Therefore, no work of expansion is done ($W = P \times 0 = 0$).
    $$q_v = \Delta E + 0$$
> **$$q_v = \Delta E$$**
*Conclusion: The heat of reaction measured at constant volume is equal to the change in internal energy ($\Delta E$) of the system.*

### Derivation 2: Heat of Reaction at Constant Pressure ($q_p = \Delta H$)
1.  When a reaction occurs at a constant pressure (like in an open beaker), the system may expand or contract, meaning work is actively done. Let this heat be denoted as $q_p$.
    $$q_p = \Delta E + P\Delta V \quad \dots \text{(Equation i)}$$
2.  To specifically study heat changes at constant pressure, the thermodynamic function **Enthalpy ($H$)** is utilized, defined as:
    $$H = E + PV$$
3.  The change in enthalpy ($\Delta H$) at constant pressure is therefore:
    $$\Delta H = \Delta E + P\Delta V \quad \dots \text{(Equation ii)}$$
4.  Comparing Equation (i) and Equation (ii), it is clearly evident that:
> **$$q_p = \Delta H$$**
*Conclusion: The heat of reaction measured at constant pressure is equal to the change in enthalpy ($\Delta H$) of the system.*

**Relationship for Gases:**
Since the Ideal Gas Law states $PV = nRT$, the pressure-volume work term can be rewritten as $P\Delta V = \Delta n_g RT$. 
Substituting this into the enthalpy equation yields the crucial relationship connecting the two conditions:
> **$$\Delta H = \Delta E + \Delta n_g RT$$**
*(Where $\Delta n_g$ is the change in the number of moles of gaseous components only).*

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes
*   **The First Law Anchor:** Whenever asked to derive these relationships, you must mathematically start with the First Law of Thermodynamics equation ($q = \Delta E + W$). Jumping straight to enthalpy definitions will lose pedagogical marks.
*   **$\Delta n_g$ Limitation:** In the final combined equation ($\Delta H = \Delta E + \Delta n_g RT$), remember that $\Delta n$ ONLY applies to the moles of strictly gaseous substances in the balanced equation. Do not count solids or liquids when calculating $\Delta n_g = n_{\text{gas(products)}} - n_{\text{gas(reactants)}}$.
