# Electrochemical Cells and Nernst
> **Topic:** Electrochemistry | **Priority:** 🟠 HIGH | **Exam Frequency:** 1/7 exams

## 📌 Quick Summary
> This section differentiates between the two major types of cells: Electrochemical cells (which produce electricity from spontaneous chemical reactions) and Electrolytic cells (which use electricity to drive non-spontaneous reactions). It also introduces the Nernst Equation, a vital thermodynamic formula for calculating the voltage of a cell under non-standard conditions.

## 📖 Core Theory

### 1. Types of Electro-Chemical Devices
There are two primary devices used in electrochemistry, distinctly defined by the direction of their energy conversion:

**A) Electrochemical Cell (Galvanic or Voltaic Cell):**
*   **Function:** A device designed to convert **chemical energy** directly into **electrical energy**.
*   **Mechanism:** It harnesses a spontaneous redox reaction ($\Delta G < 0$) to generate a flow of electrons through an external circuit. The reaction happens on its own, generating a voltage that can power external devices.
*   **Example:** A standard **Daniell Cell** (the conceptual basis for batteries). It consists of a Zinc anode in a $ZnSO_4$ solution and a Copper cathode in a $CuSO_4$ solution, connected by a salt bridge. Zinc spontaneously oxidizes, pushing electrons through a wire to reduce Copper, creating a usable current.

**B) Electrolytic Cell:**
*   **Function:** A device that converts external **electrical energy** into **chemical energy**.
*   **Mechanism:** It uses electricity from an external power source (like a battery) to force a highly non-spontaneous chemical reaction ($\Delta G > 0$) to occur. This process is called electrolysis.
*   **Example:** The **electrolysis of water** to produce Hydrogen and Oxygen gas, or the electrolysis of molten $NaCl$ to extract pure sodium metal and chlorine gas.

## 🧮 Key Derivations & Equations

### The Nernst Equation
Standard cell potentials ($E^\circ$) are strictly measured under standard conditions (ion concentrations of exactly $1 \text{ M}$, $1 \text{ atm}$ pressure, and $298 \text{ K}$). Walther Nernst derived an equation to calculate the actual cell potential ($E$) at *any* given concentration and temperature.

For a general reduction half-reaction: $M^{n+}(aq) + ne^- \rightarrow M(s)$
The Nernst equation is thermodynamically defined as:
$$E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF} \ln \frac{[\text{Reduced state (Product)}]}{[\text{Oxidized state (Reactant)}]}$$

For an entire Galvanic cell operating at standard room temperature ($298 \text{ K}$ or $25^\circ \text{C}$), substituting $R = 8.314 \text{ J/K}\cdot\text{mol}$, $F = 96485 \text{ C/mol}$, and converting the natural log ($\ln$) to base-10 log ($\log_{10}$) by multiplying by $2.303$, the equation simplifies into its most highly tested and useful form:

> **$$E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{0.0591}{n} \log \frac{[\text{Anode Ion (Oxidation)}]}{[\text{Cathode Ion (Reduction)}]}$$**

Where:
*   $n$ = Total number of moles of electrons transferred in the balanced overall redox equation.
*   Pure solids (like the metal electrodes themselves) have an activity of $1$ and are excluded from the log ratio.

## 📊 Comparison Tables

### Electrochemical vs. Electrolytic Cells
| Feature | Electrochemical (Galvanic) Cell | Electrolytic Cell |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Energy Conversion** | Chemical $\rightarrow$ Electrical | Electrical $\rightarrow$ Chemical |
| **Spontaneity** | Spontaneous redox reaction ($\Delta G < 0$) | Non-spontaneous reaction ($\Delta G > 0$) |
| **Anode Polarity** | **Negative** (Source of electrons) | **Positive** (Connected to positive terminal) |
| **Cathode Polarity** | **Positive** (Accepts electrons) | **Negative** (Connected to negative terminal) |
| **Oxidation/Reduction** | Oxidation at Anode, Reduction at Cathode | Oxidation at Anode, Reduction at Cathode |
| **Requirement** | Requires a salt bridge to maintain neutrality | Requires an external DC power source |

## 🔢 Solved Numericals

### 🎯 2024 Q.7(c) Nernst Equation Application (03 marks)
**Question:** Calculate the standard electrode potential of $Ni^{2+}/Ni$ electrode, if the cell potential of the following cell is $0.59 \text{ V}$.
$$Ni | Ni^{2+} (0.01 \text{ M}) || Cu^{2+} | Cu$$

**Given Data:**
*   Cell Potential under given conditions, $E_{cell} = 0.59 \text{ V}$
*   Concentration of Anode ion, $[Ni^{2+}] = 0.01 \text{ M}$
*   *Assumption:* Since the concentration of $Cu^{2+}$ is not explicitly given, we must assume it is at standard state: $[Cu^{2+}] = 1 \text{ M}$. Furthermore, we must use the known standard reduction potential for copper, $E^\circ_{Cu^{2+}/Cu} = +0.34 \text{ V}$.

**To Find:**
*   Standard electrode potential of Nickel, $E^\circ_{Ni^{2+}/Ni}$

**Solution:**
**Step 1: Identify the reactions and 'n'**
*   **Anode (Oxidation):** $Ni(s) \rightarrow Ni^{2+}(aq) + 2e^-$
*   **Cathode (Reduction):** $Cu^{2+}(aq) + 2e^- \rightarrow Cu(s)$
*   **Overall Reaction:** $Ni(s) + Cu^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow Ni^{2+}(aq) + Cu(s)$
Therefore, the number of electrons transferred, $n = 2$.

**Step 2: Apply the Nernst Equation**
$$E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{0.0591}{n} \log \frac{[Ni^{2+}]}{[Cu^{2+}]}$$

Substituting the known values:
$$0.59 = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{0.0591}{2} \log \left( \frac{0.01}{1} \right)$$
$$0.59 = E^\circ_{cell} - 0.02955 \log (10^{-2})$$
$$0.59 = E^\circ_{cell} - 0.02955 \times (-2)$$
$$0.59 = E^\circ_{cell} + 0.0591$$

**Step 3: Solve for $E^\circ_{cell}$**
$$E^\circ_{cell} = 0.59 - 0.0591 = 0.5309 \text{ V}$$

**Step 4: Calculate the specific $Ni$ electrode potential**
We know the formula for standard cell potential is $E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{cathode} - E^\circ_{anode}$
$$E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{Cu^{2+}/Cu} - E^\circ_{Ni^{2+}/Ni}$$
$$0.5309 = (+0.34) - E^\circ_{Ni^{2+}/Ni}$$
$$E^\circ_{Ni^{2+}/Ni} = 0.34 - 0.5309$$
$$E^\circ_{Ni^{2+}/Ni} = -0.1909 \text{ V}$$

> *Exam Typo Note:* If the examiner intended for the provided $0.59 \text{ V}$ to be the *Standard* Cell Potential ($E^\circ_{cell}$), completely ignoring the $0.01 \text{ M}$ non-standard condition, the calculation would simply be: $0.59 = 0.34 - E^\circ_{Ni^{2+}/Ni} \implies E^\circ_{Ni^{2+}/Ni} = -0.25 \text{ V}$, which perfectly matches the literature value. However, rigorously applying the Nernst equation with the data provided yields $-0.19 \text{ V}$.

**Final Answer:**
The calculated standard electrode potential of the $Ni^{2+}/Ni$ electrode is **$-0.19 \text{ V}$**.


## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes
*   **Anode/Cathode Polarity Trap:** This is the most common multiple-choice trap. In a Galvanic cell, the Anode is Negative (think "**AN**ode is **N**egative"). In an Electrolytic cell, the Anode is Positive. However, **Oxidation ALWAYS occurs at the Anode** in BOTH cells. That rule never changes.
*   **Nernst Equation Assumptions:** In Nernst calculations, remember that the concentration (activity) of pure solids (like the $Ni$ or $Cu$ metal electrodes themselves) is always exactly $1$. Only plug the concentrations of the aqueous ions into the logarithm ratio.
