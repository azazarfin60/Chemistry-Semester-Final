# Wave Mechanics
> **Topic:** Atomic Structure | **Priority:** 🟠 HIGH | **Exam Frequency:** 2/7 exams

## 📌 Quick Summary
> Wave Mechanics replaces the Bohr model by treating electrons as 3D waves rather than 2D particles. It is built upon two pillars: de Broglie's wave-particle duality ($\lambda = h/p$) and the Schrödinger wave equation, which calculates the mathematical probability of finding an electron in 3D space.

## 📖 Core Theory

### 1. Dual Nature of Matter (de Broglie's Hypothesis)
Louis de Broglie proposed that just as light exhibits wave-particle duality, all moving material particles (like electrons) also possess wave-like characteristics. The wavelength of this "matter wave" is inversely proportional to the particle's momentum. This hypothesis fundamentally dismantled Bohr's idea of a localized particle moving in a fixed path.

### 2. Schrödinger's Wave Equation
The fundamental mathematical equation of wave mechanics (quantum mechanics) that describes the 3D wave-like behavior of an electron in an atom. Proposed by Erwin Schrödinger in 1926, it relates the energy of an electron to its wave function ($\psi$).

For an electron of mass $m$ and potential energy $V$ moving in three-dimensional space ($x, y, z$), the time-independent Schrödinger wave equation is:
$$\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2} + \frac{8\pi^2m}{h^2}(E - V)\psi = 0$$

*Where:*
*   $\psi$ (psi) = wave function (amplitude of the electron wave)
*   $E$ = Total energy of the electron
*   $h$ = Planck's constant

**Physical Significance of $\psi$ and $\psi^2$:**
*   **$\psi$:** The wave function itself has no physical meaning; it merely represents the mathematical amplitude of the matter wave.
*   **$\psi^2$:** Represents **probability density**. It determines the statistical probability of finding an electron at a specific point in space. The 3D region where $\psi^2$ is maximum ($>90\%$) is what we define as an **orbital**.

## 🧮 Key Derivations & Equations

### Derivation of de-Broglie's Equation
This derivation proves the relationship between the momentum of a particle and the wavelength of its associated matter wave.

**Step 1: Planck's Quantum Theory (Wave Nature)**
The energy ($E$) of a photon of light acting as a wave is:
$$E = h\nu \quad \dots \text{(Equation 1)}$$
Since $\nu = c/\lambda$ (where $c$ is the speed of light and $\lambda$ is wavelength),
$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \quad \dots \text{(Equation 2)}$$

**Step 2: Einstein's Principle (Particle Nature)**
According to mass-energy equivalence, if the photon is considered a particle of mass $m$, its energy is:
$$E = mc^2 \quad \dots \text{(Equation 3)}$$

**Step 3: Equating the Energies**
Equating Equation 2 and Equation 3:
$$mc^2 = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

Rearranging to solve for wavelength ($\lambda$):
$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{mc^2} \implies \lambda = \frac{h}{mc}$$

**Step 4: Application to Material Particles**
For a material particle of mass $m$ moving with a velocity $v$ (instead of the speed of light $c$), we replace $c$ with $v$:
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$$

Since mass $\times$ velocity = momentum ($p$), the final boxed equation is:
> $$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

## 🏆 Golden Questions (Past Exam Archive)
### 🎯 2021 Q.5(c) Derive de-Broglie's equation for a particle of mass 'm' and moving with a velocity of 'v'. (04)
> **Source:** answers/2021.md

**Answer:**

**Derivation of de-Broglie's Equation:**
Louis de Broglie proposed that just as light exhibits wave-particle duality, all moving material particles also possess wave-like characteristics. He derived the relationship between the momentum of a particle and the wavelength of its associated wave (matter wave).

According to Planck's quantum theory, the energy ($E$) of a photon of light acting as a wave is given by:
$$E = h\nu \quad \dots \text{(Equation 1)}$$
Where $h$ is Planck's constant and $\nu$ is the frequency of the wave.
Since $\nu = c/\lambda$ (where $c$ is the speed of light and $\lambda$ is the wavelength),
$$E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \quad \dots \text{(Equation 2)}$$

According to Einstein's mass-energy equivalence principle, if the photon is considered a particle of mass $m$, its energy is:
$$E = mc^2 \quad \dots \text{(Equation 3)}$$

Equating the energy from Equation 2 and Equation 3:
$$mc^2 = \frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

Rearranging to solve for the wavelength ($\lambda$):
$$\lambda = \frac{hc}{mc^2}$$
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mc}$$

de Broglie suggested that this equation, derived for a photon, also applies to material particles. For a material particle of mass $m$ moving with a velocity $v$ (instead of the speed of light $c$), the equation becomes:
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}$$

Since mass $\times$ velocity = momentum ($p$), the equation can be written as:
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

This is the de-Broglie equation, which beautifully links the wave nature ($\lambda$) and particle nature ($p$ or $mv$) of matter.

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes
*   **Derivation Prerequisites:** When deriving de Broglie's equation, explicitly state the two starting principles: *"Planck's Theory"* and *"Einstein's Mass-Energy Equivalence"*. Writing just the math without stating the names of the source theories loses pedagogical marks.
*   **Schrödinger Equation Notation:** Be extremely careful to write the partial derivative symbol ($\partial$) instead of a regular $d$. Writing $d^2\psi/dx^2$ is mathematically incorrect for a 3D function.
