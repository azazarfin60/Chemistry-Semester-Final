# Molecular Orbital Theory
> **Topic:** Chemical Bonding | **Priority:** 🔴 CRITICAL | **Exam Frequency:** 2/7 exams

## 📌 Quick Summary
> Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) provides a quantum mechanical view of bonding where atomic orbitals combine to form completely new, polycentric molecular orbitals. Unlike Valence Bond Theory (VBT), MOT successfully explains the paramagnetic nature of $O_2$ and the stability of odd-electron molecules.

## 📖 Core Theory

### 1. Fundamentals of MOT
Developed by F. Hund and R.S. Mulliken, MOT relies on the **Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO)**.
*   **Delocalization:** Electrons in a molecule are not localized to individual atoms; they belong to the entire molecule as a whole and move in newly formed molecular orbitals (MOs).
*   **Loss of Identity:** When atomic orbitals of comparable energy and proper symmetry combine, they lose their individual identity.
*   **Conservation of Orbitals:** The number of molecular orbitals formed is exactly equal to the number of atomic orbitals combining.

### 2. Types of Molecular Orbitals
When two atomic orbitals combine, they form two distinct molecular orbitals:
1.  **Bonding Molecular Orbital (BMO):** Formed by the *additive* combination of wave functions. It has **lower energy** than the isolated atomic orbitals and provides high stability to the molecule. The electron density is concentrated between the nuclei, pulling them together.
2.  **Antibonding Molecular Orbital (ABMO):** Formed by the *subtractive* combination of wave functions (denoted by an asterisk, e.g., $\sigma^*$). It has **higher energy** and lower stability. The electron density is concentrated away from the internuclear region, pushing the nuclei apart.

### 3. Rules for Filling MOs
Just like atomic orbitals, the filling of electrons into molecular orbitals strictly obeys three fundamental rules:
*   **Aufbau Principle:** Orbitals with lower energy (BMOs) are filled first before higher energy ones (ABMOs).
*   **Pauli Exclusion Principle:** A single MO can hold a maximum of 2 electrons, and they must have opposite spins.
*   **Hund's Rule:** Degenerate orbitals (orbitals with the exact same energy, like $\pi_{2px}$ and $\pi_{2py}$) are singly occupied before pairing occurs.

## 🧮 Key Derivations & Equations
The stability of a molecule in MOT is determined by its **Bond Order (B.O.)**:
**$$B.O. = \frac{N_b - N_a}{2}$$**
*   $N_b$ = Total number of electrons in Bonding Molecular Orbitals
*   $N_a$ = Total number of electrons in Antibonding Molecular Orbitals
*(A bond order of 0 or negative means the molecule does not exist).*

## 📊 Comparison Tables

### Valence Bond Theory (VBT) vs. Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)
| Feature | Valence Bond Theory (VBT) | Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Electron Localization** | Electrons are localized in the specific atomic orbitals of the interacting atoms. | Electrons are delocalized; they belong to the entire molecule as a whole. |
| **Identity of Atoms** | Participating atoms retain their individual identity after bond formation. | Participating atoms lose their individual identity. |
| **Orbital Interaction** | Overlapping of half-filled atomic orbitals. | Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO) to form BMOs and ABMOs. |
| **Resonance/Hybrids** | Uses hybridization and resonance to explain complex geometries. | Does not require resonance or hybridization to explain structure. |
| **Magnetic Properties** | Fails to predict the paramagnetic nature of $O_2$ (predicts it as diamagnetic). | Successfully explains magnetic properties (e.g., $O_2$ is paramagnetic due to unpaired electrons in ABMOs). |
| **Odd-Electron Species**| Struggles to explain stability of odd-electron molecules ($NO$, $O_2^-$). | Easily explains odd-electron stability via calculated bond orders. |

### 🎯 2023 Q.3(a) State and explain molecular orbital theory (MOT). (03)
> **Source:** answers/2023.md

**Answer:**

**Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT):**
Developed by F. Hund and R.S. Mulliken, MOT provides a quantum mechanical explanation for chemical bonding. The key postulates (statements) are:

1.  **Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals (LCAO):** Just as electrons of atoms are present in atomic orbitals, the electrons of a molecule are present in new orbitals called molecular orbitals (MOs). These MOs are formed by the linear combination (addition and subtraction of wave functions) of atomic orbitals of comparable energy and proper symmetry.
2.  **Number of MOs:** The number of molecular orbitals formed is exactly equal to the number of atomic orbitals combining. Two atomic orbitals combine to form two MOs:
    *   **Bonding Molecular Orbital (BMO):** Formed by the addition of wave functions. It has lower energy and greater stability than the combining atomic orbitals.
    *   **Antibonding Molecular Orbital (ABMO):** Formed by the subtraction of wave functions. It has higher energy and lower stability.
3.  **Loss of Identity:** When atomic orbitals merge to form molecular orbitals, they lose their individual identity. The new MO is polycentric, meaning the electron cloud is influenced by two or more nuclei.
4.  **Filling Rules:** The filling of electrons into these molecular orbitals strictly follows the Aufbau principle (increasing energy order), Pauli's exclusion principle (max 2 electrons per MO with opposite spins), and Hund's rule of maximum multiplicity.

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes
*   **The VBT vs MOT Trump Card:** The most frequently tested aspect of MOT is distinguishing it from VBT. If asked to compare them, you **must** highlight the "Magnetic Properties" row. Pointing out that VBT fails on $O_2$ while MOT succeeds is the crucial discriminator examiners look for.
*   **LCAO Acronym:** Always explicitly write out "Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals" before using the LCAO acronym in your postulates.
*   **Bonding vs Antibonding:** Ensure you clearly state that BMOs are formed by *addition* (constructive interference) and have *lower* energy, while ABMOs are formed by *subtraction* (destructive interference) and have *higher* energy.
