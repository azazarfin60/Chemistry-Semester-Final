# Kirchhoffs Equation
> **Topic:** Thermochemistry | **Priority:** 🟠 HIGH | **Exam Frequency:** ?/7 exams

## 📌 Quick Summary
> The heat of a reaction is not a strict constant; it varies depending on the temperature at which it is carried out. Kirchhoff's Equation mathematically describes this variation, proving that the change in enthalpy depends directly on the difference between the heat capacities ($\Delta C_p$) of the products and the reactants.

## 📖 Core Theory

### 1. Effect of Temperature on Heat of Reaction
The heat of a reaction ($\Delta H$) does not remain constant if the ambient temperature changes. This variation occurs fundamentally because the heat capacity (the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a substance by $1^\circ C$) of the products is almost never identical to the heat capacity of the reactants. 

### 2. Kirchhoff's Equation
Kirchhoff's equation mathematically quantifies this temperature dependence. It states that the change in the heat of reaction for every one-degree change in temperature is exactly equal to the difference in the heat capacities of the products and reactants at constant pressure ($\Delta C_p$).

$$\frac{\Delta H_2 - \Delta H_1}{T_2 - T_1} = \Delta C_p$$

Where:
*   $\Delta C_p = \sum (C_p)_{\text{products}} - \sum (C_p)_{\text{reactants}}$
*   $T_1, T_2$ = Initial and final temperatures in Kelvin ($K$).
*   $\Delta H_1, \Delta H_2$ = Heat of reaction at $T_1$ and $T_2$ respectively.

**Interpretation of the Equation:**
1.  **If $\Delta C_p$ is positive:** The heat capacity of products > reactants. Therefore, the heat of reaction ($\Delta H$) will **increase** as temperature increases.
2.  **If $\Delta C_p$ is negative:** The heat capacity of products < reactants. Therefore, the heat of reaction ($\Delta H$) will **decrease** as temperature increases.
3.  **If $\Delta C_p$ is zero:** The heat capacity of products exactly equals reactants. The heat of reaction remains completely independent of temperature.

## 🔢 Solved Numericals

### 🎯 2021 Q.7(b) Kirchhoff's Application (04 marks)
**Question:** The heat of the reaction: $\frac{1}{2}H_2 + \frac{1}{2}Cl_2 \rightarrow HCl$ at $27^\circ C$ is $-22.1 \text{ kcal}$. Calculate the heat of reaction at $77^\circ C$. The molar heat capacities at constant pressure at $27^\circ C$ for hydrogen, chlorine and HCl are: 6.82, 7.70, 6.80 $\text{cal mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$ respectively.

**Given Data:**
*   Reaction: $\frac{1}{2}H_2 + \frac{1}{2}Cl_2 \rightarrow HCl$
*   Temperatures: $T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300 \text{ K}$, $T_2 = 77 + 273 = 350 \text{ K}$
*   Initial Enthalpy: $\Delta H_1 = -22.1 \text{ kcal} = -22100 \text{ cal}$
*   Heat Capacities: $C_p(H_2) = 6.82$, $C_p(Cl_2) = 7.70$, $C_p(HCl) = 6.80 \text{ cal/K mol}$

**Step 1: Calculate $\Delta C_p$ with Stoichiometry**
$$\Delta C_p = \sum C_p(\text{Products}) - \sum C_p(\text{Reactants})$$
$$\Delta C_p = C_p(HCl) - \left[ \frac{1}{2} C_p(H_2) + \frac{1}{2} C_p(Cl_2) \right]$$
$$\Delta C_p = 6.80 - \left[ \frac{1}{2}(6.82) + \frac{1}{2}(7.70) \right]$$
$$\Delta C_p = 6.80 - [3.41 + 3.85] = 6.80 - 7.26$$
$$\Delta C_p = -0.46 \text{ cal/K mol}$$

**Step 2: Apply Kirchhoff's Equation**
$$\frac{\Delta H_2 - \Delta H_1}{T_2 - T_1} = \Delta C_p$$
$$\Delta H_2 - (-22100) = -0.46 \times (350 - 300)$$
$$\Delta H_2 + 22100 = -0.46 \times 50$$
$$\Delta H_2 + 22100 = -23$$
$$\Delta H_2 = -22123 \text{ cal} = \mathbf{-22.123 \text{ kcal}}$$

## ⚡ Exam Tips & Common Mistakes
*   **The Stoichiometry Trap:** When calculating $\Delta C_p$ in numericals, you **MUST** multiply the given molar heat capacities by their exact stoichiometric coefficients from the balanced equation (e.g., multiplying by $1/2$ for $H_2$ in the 2021 question). This is the #1 place students lose marks.
*   **The Unit Trap (kCal vs Cal):** Heat capacities ($C_p$) are almost always given in `cal`, while the initial enthalpy $\Delta H_1$ is given in `kcal`. Always convert $\Delta H_1$ to `cal` (multiply by 1000) *before* plugging it into Kirchhoff's equation!
