# Slide 26

![Henry's Law Applications and Limitations](Solution-026.png)

## **Henry’s Law**

### **Applications of Henry’s Law**
*   Production of carbonated beverages is based on Henry’s law.
*   Hypoxia (concentration of oxygen is low in blood and tissues) can be explained by Henry’s law.
*   It is applied in deep sea diving as well.
*   We can understand by Henry’s law that aquatic life survives better in cold water.

### **Limitations of Henry’s law**
*   It is applicable when the system is in equilibrium.
*   It is applicable for those gases only which do not react with water(solvent).
*   Gas shouldn’t cause any chemical change in the solution.
*   Henry’s law is appropriate only when pressure is not high, temperature is not too low and gas is extremely soluble.

> [!NOTE]
> *Note on Solubility Limitation:* While the slide states the law is appropriate when the gas is "extremely soluble," in standard chemical theory, Henry's law is most accurate for **sparingly soluble** gases that do not interact strongly with the solvent.

# Slide 27

![Application of Henry's Law - Soda Can](Solution-027.png)

## **Application of Henry’s Law**

### **Why Does Your Can of Soda Fizz Out When Opened?**

**Pedagogical Analysis of Diagram:**
This diagram provides a clear mechanical explanation for the "fizz" in carbonated drinks:
- **Closed Can (Left):** The $CO_2$ in the headspace is under high pressure. According to Henry's Law, this high partial pressure keeps a large amount of $CO_2$ dissolved in the liquid.
- **Opened Can (Right):** Once the tab is pulled, the high-pressure $CO_2$ escapes into the atmosphere (pressure released).
- **Result:** The sudden drop in partial pressure above the liquid drastically reduces the solubility of the dissolved $CO_2$. To reach a new equilibrium, the gas rapidly escapes from the solution, forming the characteristic bubbles and "fizz."

# Slide 28

![Application of Henry's Law - Scuba Diving](Solution-028.png)

## **Application of Henry’s Law**

*   $\checkmark$ Sealing of soft drink bottles at high pressure increases the solubility of $CO_2$ in soft drinks and soda water.
*   $\checkmark$ When a diver goes underwater his body is subjected to high pressure. Due to this the body tissues absorbs more gases. The breathed in oxygen is required for cellular respiration to provide energy to the diver. But the nitrogen gets absorbed into the body tissues.
*   $\checkmark$ To avoid **bends** (due to blockage of capillaries) and the toxic effects of high concentrations of nitrogen in the blood, the tanks used by scuba divers are filled with air diluted with helium (**11.7% helium, 56.2% nitrogen and 32.1% oxygen**).

### **Pedagogical Analysis of Diagram:**
- **Diver Image:** The cartoon illustrates a scuba diver at depth, where the ambient pressure is significantly higher than at the surface.
- **The "Bends":** The text explains that at depth, nitrogen (which is normally insoluble at surface pressure) dissolves into the blood and tissues. If the diver surfaces too quickly, this nitrogen rapidly comes out of solution as bubbles (just like the soda can diagram shown on the right), causing decompression sickness or "the bends."
- **Solution:** Diluting the breathing gas with **Helium** (which has much lower solubility in blood than nitrogen) helps prevent this dangerous condition.
