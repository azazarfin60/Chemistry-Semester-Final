# Slide 22

![Solubility vs Temperature Graph 1](Solution-022.png)

## **Factors Affecting Solubility**

### **Solubility vs. Temperature for a variety of salts**

**Pedagogical Analysis of Graph:**
This graph plots solubility (grams of solute per 100g of water) against temperature ($^\circ C$) for five different salts, illustrating diverse thermal behaviors:
1.  **$Na_2SO_4$ (Sodium Sulfate):** Shows a unique behavior where solubility increases sharply until about $33^\circ C$ and then begins to decrease. This is due to the change from the hydrated form ($Na_2SO_4 \cdot 10H_2O$) to the anhydrous form.
2.  **$NaCl$ (Sodium Chloride):** The line is nearly horizontal, indicating that temperature has a very minimal effect on its solubility.
3.  **$Na_2HAsO_4$ (Sodium Arsenate):** Shows a very steep, rapid increase in solubility as temperature rises.
4.  **$Ba(NO_3)_2$ (Barium Nitrate):** Shows a steady, linear increase.
5.  **$Ce_2(SO_4)_3 \cdot 9H_2O$ (Cerium Sulfate):** Shows a **decrease** in solubility with increasing temperature, a rare case for solid solutes.

# Slide 23

![Solubility vs Temperature Graph 2](Solution-023.png)

## **Factors Affecting Solubility**

- **The faster the water molecules are moving, the more collisions occur with the salt ions.**
- **The warmer the temperature, the more kinetic energy the water molecules have to pull ions away.**

### **Pedagogical Analysis of Graph:**
This graph provides a broader range of salts ($NaNO_3, CaCl_2, Pb(NO_3)_2, KNO_3, NaCl, KCl, K_2Cr_2O_7, KClO_3, Ce_2(SO_4)_3$).
- **Mechanism:** The text explains the kinetic molecular theory behind solubility. Increased temperature leads to higher kinetic energy of solvent molecules, causing more frequent and energetic collisions with the solute lattice, which facilitates the "pulling away" of ions into the solution.
- **Exception Note:** The curve for $Ce_2(SO_4)_3$ clearly trends downward, highlighting that while most solids dissolve endothermically (absorbing heat), some dissolve exothermically, making them less soluble at higher temperatures.

# Slide 24

![Gas Solubility vs Temperature](Solution-024.png)

## **Factors Affecting Solubility**

- **The solubility of gases decrease with increasing temperatures.**
- **But, why?**
- **Non-polar gases have very little attraction to polar liquids.**
- **Higher temperatures means more kinetic energy to escape the already weak intermolecular forces.**

### **Pedagogical Analysis of Graph:**
The graph plots the solubility (in mM) of various gases ($CH_4, O_2, CO, He$) against temperature.
- **Observation:** All curves show a clear downward trend.
- **Concept:** Unlike most solids, the dissolution of gases in liquids is typically an exothermic process. Adding heat (increasing temperature) provides gas molecules with enough kinetic energy to overcome the weak attractive forces of the solvent and escape back into the gas phase. This is why "flat" soda or low-oxygen water in warm ponds occurs.

# Slide 25

![Henry's Law](Solution-025.png)

## **Henry’s Law**

At a constant temperature, the amount of a given gas that dissolves in a given type and volume of liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas in equilibrium with that liquid.

### **Mathematical Form:**
$$W = kp$$
Where:
- $W$ = concentration of dissolved gas at equilibrium
- $p$ = partial pressure of the gas
- $k$ = Henry’s law constant

### **Pedagogical Analysis of Diagrams:**
The three-part diagram illustrates the effect of pressure on gas solubility:
- **(a) Dynamic equilibrium:** Gas particles are distributed between the headspace and the liquid. The rate of gas entering the liquid equals the rate of gas escaping.
- **(b) $P_{gas}$ increased:** A piston compresses the gas, increasing its partial pressure. This forces more gas collisions with the liquid surface.
- **(c) New Dynamic equilibrium:** To restore equilibrium, more gas molecules dissolve into the liquid until the rates again equalize. This visually confirms that higher pressure leads to higher gas solubility.
- **Historical Context:** Includes a portrait of **William Henry**, the British chemist who formulated this law in 1803.
