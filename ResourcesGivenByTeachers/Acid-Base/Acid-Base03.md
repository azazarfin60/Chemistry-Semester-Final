## Slide 11: Usanovich Concept

**Definition:**
According to this concept, an **acid** is any chemical species which is capable of combining with anions or electrons or giving up cations. Conversely, a **base** is any chemical species which is capable of giving up anions or electrons or combining with cations.

This definition includes all Lewis acids and bases and all redox reactions which may consist of the complete transfer of one or more electrons.

### Examples of Usanovich Acid-Base Reactions:

| Acid-Base Reactions | Explanation |
| :--- | :--- |
| $SO_3 + Na_2O \rightarrow Na_2SO_4$ | **Base:** $Na_2O$ gives up $O^{2-}$ ion; **Acid:** $SO_3$ combines with $O^{2-}$ ion. <br> $Na_2O \rightarrow 2Na^+ + O^{2-}$ <br> $SO_3 + O^{2-} \rightarrow SO_4^{2-}$ <br> $2Na^+ + SO_4^{2-} \rightarrow Na_2SO_4$ (salt) |
| $Fe(CN)_2 + 4KCN \rightarrow K_4[Fe(CN)_6]$ | **Acid:** $Fe(CN)_2$ combines with $CN^-$ ion; **Base:** $KCN$ gives up $CN^-$ ion. <br> $4KCN \rightarrow 4K^+ + 4CN^-$ <br> $Fe(CN)_2 + 4CN^- \rightarrow [Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$ <br> $4K^+ + [Fe(CN)_6]^{4-} \rightarrow K_4[Fe(CN)_6]$ |
| $Cl_2 + 2Na \rightarrow 2NaCl$ | **Base:** $Na$ atom loses an electron; **Acid:** $Cl$ combines with this electron. <br> $2Na \rightarrow 2Na^+ + 2e^-$ <br> $2Cl + 2e^- \rightarrow 2Cl^-$ <br> $2Na^+ + 2Cl^- \rightarrow 2NaCl$ |

### Utility of the Concept:
This concept is particularly advantageous in classifying together all examples of acids and bases. It is the most general of all acid-base concepts.

### Limitations:
1. The concept is so broad that it becomes extremely general, essentially a synthesis of all previous concepts.
2. It includes many reactions (like redox) that might be better categorized separately.
3. It virtually considers all chemical reactions as acid-base reactions.

## Slide 12: Lux-Flood Concept

**Definitions:**
- **Acid:** An oxide ion ($O^{2-}$) acceptor (e.g., $SiO_2, CO_2, P_4O_{10}, SO_3, Na_2S_2O_7$).
- **Base:** An oxide ion ($O^{2-}$) donor (e.g., $CaO, PbO, BaO, Na_2O, TiO_2$).

**Amphoteric Substances:**
Substances that can act as both oxide donors and acceptors:
- $ZnO \rightleftharpoons Zn^{2+} + O^{2-}$ (Base)
- $ZnO + O^{2-} \rightleftharpoons ZnO_2^{2-}$ (Acid)
- $Al_2O_3 \rightleftharpoons 2AlO_2^-$ (Acid behavior)

## Lewis Concept

**Definitions:**
- **Acid:** Any species (molecule, radical, or ion) which can **accept** an electron pair.
  - Examples: $H^+, RCO^+, Co^{3+}, Fe^{3+}, CO_2, BF_3, SO_2$
- **Base:** Any species (molecule, radical, or ion) which can **donate** an electron pair.
  - Examples: $OH^-, F^-, NH_3, H_2O$

### Utility of Lewis Concept:
1. Includes reactions where no protons are involved.
2. More general than Bronsted-Lowry as it doesn't depend on a specific element (like Hydrogen) or the presence of a solvent.
3. Explains basic properties of metallic oxides and acidic properties of non-metallic oxides.

---

## Slide 13: Utility and Limitations of Lewis Concept (Continued)

### Utility (Continued):
4. Includes gas phase, high temperature, and non-solvent reactions as neutralization processes.
5. Invaluable where the protonic concept is inapplicable, such as reactions between acidic and basic oxides in a fused state.

### Limitations:
1. **Relative Strength:** It is not possible to arrange Lewis acids/bases in a universal order of strength because strength depends on the specific reaction.
   - Example: $Be^{2+}$ is more acidic than $Cu^{2+}$ towards fluoride, but $Cu^{2+}$ is more acidic than $Be^{2+}$ towards amines.
2. **Reaction Speed:** While acid-base reactions are typically rapid, many Lewis reactions are slow.

## Slide 14: Classification of Lewis Acids

Lewis acids must have at least one empty orbital in the valence shell. They are classified as:

### 1. Molecules with an Incomplete Octet:
Electron-deficient molecules like alkyls and halides of $Be, B,$ and $Al$.
**Examples (Adduct Formation):**
- $F_3B + :O(C_2H_5)_2 \rightarrow F_3B \leftarrow O(C_2H_5)_2$
- $Cl_3Al + :NC_5H_5 \rightarrow Cl_3Al \leftarrow NC_5H_5$
- $Me_3B + :N_2H_4 \rightarrow Me_3B \leftarrow N_2H_4$

### 2. Molecules with Vacant d-orbitals:
Central atoms in halides like $SiX_4, GeX_4, TiCl_4, SnX_4, PF_5, SF_4, TeCl_4$ have vacant d-orbitals and can accept electron pairs to form adducts. These are often vigorously hydrolyzed by $H_2O$.

### 3. Simple Cations:
All simple cations are potential Lewis acids.
**Examples:**
- **Ammonation:** $Ag^+ + 2(:NH_3) \rightarrow [Ag(NH_3)_2]^+$
- **Hydration:** $Co^{2+} + 6(:H_2O) \rightarrow [Co(H_2O)_6]^{2+}$
- **Alcoholation:** $Li^+ + :O(CH_3)_2 \rightarrow [Li \leftarrow O(CH_3)_2]^+$

**Trends in Cationic Lewis Acidity:** Strength increases with:
(a) Increase in positive charge.
(b) Increase in nuclear charge (across a period).
(c) Decrease in ionic radius.
(d) Decrease in shielding electron shells.

## Slide 15: Classification of Lewis Acids (Continued)

### 3. Simple Cations (Trends Continued):
Acid strength increases from left to right in a period and bottom to top in a group.
- $Fe^{2+} < Fe^{3+}$ (Charge effect)
- $K^+ < Na^+$ (Group effect)
- $Li^+ < Be^{2+}$ (Period effect)

### 4. Molecules with Multiple Bonds between Dissimilar Electronegativity:
In molecules like $CO_2, SO_2, SO_3$, the O-atom is more electronegative, making the C or S atom electron-deficient and capable of accepting an electron pair.
**Examples:**
- $O=C=O + :OH^- \rightarrow [HO-CO_2]^-$ (Bicarbonate ion)
- $O=S=O + :OH^- \rightarrow [HO-SO_2]^-$ (Bisulphite ion)

### 5. Elements with an Electron Sextet:
Oxygen and Sulfur atoms (6 valence electrons) act as Lewis acids.
**Examples (Oxidation as Acid-Base):**
- $SO_3^{2-} (\text{Base}) + :O (\text{Acid}) \rightarrow SO_4^{2-}$
- $SO_3^{2-} (\text{Base}) + :S (\text{Acid}) \rightarrow S_2O_3^{2-}$
