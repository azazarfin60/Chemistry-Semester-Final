## Slide 01: Arrhenius Concept of Water Ion System

**Definition:**
According to this concept, an acid is any hydrogen-containing compound which gives $H^+$ ions in aqueous solution, and a base is one which gives $OH^-$ ions in aqueous solution. Thus, $HCl$ is an acid and $NaOH$ is a base, and the neutralization process can be represented by a reaction involving the combination of $H^+$ and $OH^-$ ions to form $H_2O$.

### Utility:
1. Since the reaction representing the neutralization process involves the combination of $H^+$ and $OH^-$ ions, an approximately constant molar heat of neutralization would be expected. Thus, the constant heat of neutralization of a strong acid by a strong base is readily understandable in terms of this concept.
2. This concept has offered a means of correlating catalytic behavior with the concentration of the $H^+$ ion.

### Limitations:
1. According to this concept, $HCl$ is regarded as an acid only when dissolved in $H_2O$ and not in some other solvent such as $C_6H_6$ or when it exists in the gaseous form.
2. It cannot account for the acidic and basic character of materials in non-aqueous solvents; e.g., $NH_4NO_3$ in liquid $NH_3$ acts as an acid, though it does not give $H^+$ ions. Similarly, many organic materials and $NH_3$, which do not have $OH^-$ ions at all, are actually known to show basic character.
3. The neutralization process is limited to those reactions which can occur in aqueous solutions only, although reactions involving salt formation do occur in many other solvents and even in the absence of solvents.
4. It cannot explain the acidic character of certain salts such as $AlCl_3$ in aqueous solution.

## Slide 02: Bronsted-Lowry Concept (Proton-Donor Acceptor System)
**(Page: 687)**

**Definition:**
According to this concept, an **acid** is defined as any hydrogen-containing material (a molecule, a cation, or an anion) that can release a proton ($H^+$) to any other substance. A **base** is a substance (a molecule, a cation, or an anion) which can accept a proton from any other substance.

In short:
- **Acid:** Proton donor
- **Base:** Proton acceptor

**Example:**
Consider the reaction between $HCl$ and $H_2O$:

### Diagram: Proton Transfer in $HCl$ and $H_2O$
The diagram illustrates the reversible reaction:
$$HCl + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + Cl^-$$

- **Forward Reaction:** A line with an arrow indicates "one proton ($H^+$) going to $H_2O$". $HCl$ acts as the **Acid**, and $H_2O$ acts as the **Base**.
- **Backward Reaction:** A line with an arrow indicates "one proton ($H^+$) going to $Cl^-$". $H_3O^+$ acts as the **Acid**, and $Cl^-$ acts as the **Base**.

**Analysis:**
In this reaction, $HCl$ donates a proton to $H_2O$ and is, therefore, an acid. Water, on the other hand, accepts a proton from $HCl$ and is, therefore, a base. In the reverse reaction, which at equilibrium proceeds at the same rate as the forward reaction, the $H_3O^+$ ion donates a proton to the $Cl^-$ ion; hence, $H_3O^+$ is an acid. The $Cl^-$ ion, because it accepts a proton from $H_3O^+$, is a base.

### Examples of Bronsted-Lowry Acids and Bases:

| Type of Species | Acids | Bases |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Molecular** | $HCl, HBr, HClO_4, HNO_3, H_2SO_4, H_3PO_4, H_2O$ | $NH_3, N_2H_4, \text{amines}, H_2O, NH_2OH$ |
| **Cationic** | $NH_4^+, [Fe(H_2O)_6]^{3+}, [Al(H_2O)_6]^{3+}, H_3O^+$ | $[Fe(H_2O)_5(OH)]^{2+}, [Al(H_2O)_5(OH)]^{2+}$ |
| **Anionic** | $HCO_3^-, HSO_4^-$ | $Cl^-, Br^-, I^-, OH^-, CO_3^{2-}, SO_4^{2-}, H^-, NH_2^-, OH^-, HS^-$ |

## Slide 03: Conjugate Acid-Base Pairs

**Concept:**
In an acid-base reaction, the acid ($HA$) gives up its proton ($H^+$) and produces a new base ($A^-$). The new base that is related to the original acid is called a **conjugate base** (meaning "related").

Similarly, the original base ($B^-$) after accepting a proton ($H^+$) gives a new acid ($HB$), which is called a **conjugate acid**.

### Diagram: Conjugate Pairs
The diagram shows the general reaction:
$$HA (\text{Acid}) + B^- (\text{Base}) \rightleftharpoons HB (\text{Conjugate acid}) + A^- (\text{Conjugate base})$$

- A bracket labeled **"Conjugate pair"** connects $HA$ (Acid) and $A^-$ (Conjugate base).
- Another bracket labeled **"Conjugate pair"** connects $B^-$ (Base) and $HB$ (Conjugate acid).

**Definition:**
The acid ($HA$) and the conjugate base ($A^-$) that are related to each other by donating and accepting a single proton are said to constitute a **conjugate acid-base pair**.

## Slide 04: An Important Rule

**Rule:**
It has been observed that acid-base reactions proceed in a direction to form the weak acid and weak base. Hence, in these reactions, a strong acid reacts with a strong base to form a weak acid and a weak base.

Consequently, in these reactions, the strong acid and weak base form one conjugate acid-base pair, and similarly, a strong base and a weak acid form another conjugate acid-base pair.

**Example 1:**
$$HCl + H_2O \rightleftharpoons Cl^- + H_3O^+$$
Since $HCl$ is a strong acid, its conjugate base, $Cl^-$, is a weak base. Thus, $HCl$ (strong acid) and $Cl^-$ (weak base) form one conjugate pair. Similarly, $H_3O^+$ (stronger acid) and $H_2O$ (weak base) form another conjugate pair.

**Example 2:**
### Diagram: Acetic Acid and Water
The diagram illustrates the reaction:
$$CH_3COOH (\text{Weak acid}) + H_2O (\text{Weak base}) \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ (\text{Stronger acid}) + CH_3COO^- (\text{Stronger base})$$

- Brackets connect $CH_3COOH$ with $CH_3COO^-$ and $H_2O$ with $H_3O^+$, both labeled **"Conjugate pair"**.
- **Caption:** Figure 27.5: A weak acid ($CH_3COOH$) produces a strong conjugate base ($CH_3COO^-$); and a weaker base ($H_2O$) gives a stronger conjugate acid ($H_3O^+$).

**Conclusion:**
The conjugate base of a strong acid is always a weak base, and the conjugate acid of a strong base is always a weak acid.

### Protocity of an Acid:
The number of $H^+$ (or $H_3O^+$) ions liberated by one molecule of an acid in aqueous solution is known as its **basicity**.
- **Monoprotic:** $HCl, HNO_3$ (liberate one $H^+$)
- **Diprotic:** $H_2SO_4, H_2CO_3$ (liberate two $H^+$)
- **Triprotic:** $H_3PO_4$ (liberate three $H^+$)

### Hydroxity of a Base:
The number of $OH^-$ ions liberated by one molecule of a base in aqueous solution is known as its **hydroxity**.
- **Monohydroxic:** $NaOH, NH_4OH$ (liberate one $OH^-$)
- **Dihydroxic:** $Ca(OH)_2, Mg(OH)_2$ (liberate two $OH^-$)
- **Trihydroxic:** $Al(OH)_3, Fe(OH)_3$ (liberate three $OH^-$)

## Slide 05: Relative Strength of Acids and Bases

**General Principles:**
Acids and bases are electrolytes and ionize in water. Acids dissociate to give $H^+$ or $H_3O^+$ ions, and bases ionize to give $OH^-$ ions.

The **strength** of an acid or a base is defined as its capacity to give $H^+$ and $OH^-$ ions, respectively, in equimolar aqueous solutions. Relative strengths can be compared by measuring the concentration of these ions.

- **Strong Acids/Bases:** Ionize almost completely (e.g., Acids: $HNO_3, H_2SO_4, HClO_4, HCl, HBr, HI$; Bases: $NaOH, KOH, (CH_3)_4NOH$).
- **Weak Acids/Bases:** Ionized only to a limited extent.

### Acid Dissociation Constant ($K_a$):
For an aqueous solution of an acid $HA$:
$$HA + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_3O^+ + A^-$$
The equilibrium constant $K$ is:
$$K = \frac{[H_3O^+][A^-]}{[HA][H_2O]}$$
Since $[H_2O]$ is constant in dilute solutions:
$$K_a = K \times [H_2O] = \frac{[H_3O^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$$
**Conclusion:** A larger $K_a$ value indicates a higher concentration of $H_3O^+$ ions and, therefore, a stronger acid.

### Base Dissociation Constant ($K_b$):
For a weak base $B$:
$$B + H_2O \rightleftharpoons BH^+ + OH^-$$
The base dissociation constant is:
$$K_b = \frac{[BH^+][OH^-]}{[B]}$$
**Conclusion:** A larger $K_b$ value indicates a higher concentration of $OH^-$ ions and, therefore, a stronger base.
