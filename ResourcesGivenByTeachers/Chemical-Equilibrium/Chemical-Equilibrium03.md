# Chemical Equilibrium - Slides 11-15

## Slide 11
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-011

### Text Content
**Relation Between Kp, Kc and Kx**

Consider a general gaseous reaction
$$aA + bB + \dots \rightleftharpoons lL + mM + \dots$$

According to law of mass action, we have,
$$Kc = \frac{(L)_e^l \times (M)_e^m \times \dots}{(A)_e^a \times (B)_e^b \times \dots} \dots (i)$$

The active mass of the substance can be expressed as partial pressure $p$ in case of gaseous substances, then equilibrium constant denoted as Kp will be
$$Kp = \frac{(p_L)_e^l \times (p_M)_e^m \times \dots}{(p_A)_e^a \times (p_B)_e^b \times \dots} \dots (ii)$$

The active mass of the substance can also be expressed as mole fraction, then equilibrium constant denoted as Kx will be
$$Kx = \frac{(X_L)_e^l \times (X_M)_e^m \times \dots}{(X_A)_e^a \times (X_B)_e^b \times \dots} \dots (iii)$$

## Slide 12
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-012

### Text Content
**Relation Between Kp, Kc and Kc**

> [!NOTE]
> The slide title contains a typo ("Kc" repeated). Based on the context of the derivation, it refers to the relationship between $K_p$ and $K_c$.

For ideal gas mixture, if the partial pressure of $n$ mole of any gas is $p$, and if the gas occupied volume $V$ at temperature $T$, then,
$$pV = nRT$$
$$Or, p = \frac{n}{V} RT = cRT \dots (iv)$$
[where $c$ = molar concentration]

Putting the value of $p$ into eq (ii), we get,
$$Kp = \frac{(L)_e^l \times (M)_e^m \times \dots (RT)^{l+m}}{(A)_e^a \times (B)_e^b \times \dots (RT)^{a+b}}$$
$$Kp = Kc \times (RT)^{\Delta n} \dots (v)$$

[Here, $\Delta n = (l + m + \dots) - (a + b + \dots)$]

Again, the partial pressure $p$ of a gas and total pressure $P$ of a mixture of gas, then,
$$p = xP$$
[$x$ = mole fraction]

## Slide 13
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-013

### Text Content
**Relation Between Kp, Kc and Kc**

Putting the value of $p$ into eq -(ii), we get,
$$Kp = \frac{(X_L)_e^l \times (X_M)_e^m \times \dots (P)^{l+m}}{(X_A)_e^a \times (X_B)_e^b \times \dots (P)^{a+b}}$$

Using eq- (iii), we get,
$$Kp = Kx \times P^{\Delta n} \dots (vi)$$

[Here, $\Delta n = (l + m + \dots) - (a + b + \dots)$]

From eq- v and vi, we get,
$$Kc \times (RT)^{\Delta n} = Kx \times P^{\Delta n}$$
$$Or, Kc = Kx \times (P/RT)^{\Delta n} = Kx (1/V)^{\Delta n} \dots (vii)$$

Re-arranging eq- vii, we get,
$$Kx = KcV^{\Delta n} \dots (viii)$$

## Slide 14
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-014

### Text Content
**Relation Between Kp, Kc and Kc**

If $\Delta n = 0$, then we get,
- $Kp = Kc$
- $Kp = Kx$
- and $Kx = Kc$

Hence, **$Kp = Kx = Kc$**

## Slide 15
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-015

### Text Content
**Equilibrium Constant Expression**

Write the equilibrium equation for each of the following reactions:
(a) $CO_2(g) + C(s) \rightleftharpoons 2CO(g)$
(b) $Hg(l) + Hg^{2+}(aq) \rightleftharpoons Hg_2^{2+}(aq)$

**STRATEGY**
Write the usual equilibrium constant expressions but **omit the pure solid carbon** in part (a) and the **pure liquid mercury** in part (b) because the ratio of their concentrations to their concentrations in the standard state is equal to 1.

**SOLUTION**
(a) $$K_c = \frac{[CO]^2}{[CO_2]}$$
Alternatively, because $CO$ and $CO_2$ are gases, the equilibrium equation can be written using partial pressures:
$$K_p = \frac{(P_{CO})^2}{P_{CO_2}}$$
The relationship between $K_p$ and $K_c$ is $K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n} = K_c(RT)$, because $\Delta n = 2 - 1 = 1$.

(b) $$K_c = \frac{[Hg_2^{2+}]}{[Hg^{2+}]}$$
In this case, it's not appropriate to write an expression for $K_p$ because none of the reactants and products is a gas.
