# Chemical Equilibrium - Slides 01-05

## Slide 01
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-001

### Text Content
**Chemical Equilibrium**

Figure 3. When a soft drink is opened, several equilibrium shifts occur. (credit: modification of work by "D Coetzee"/Flickr)

### Diagram Analysis: Soft Drink Equilibrium
- **Visual Description**: A photograph of a dark soft drink in a conical flask, showing significant effervescence (bubbling).
- **Pedagogical Significance**: The image illustrates the transition of dissolved $CO_2$ from the liquid phase to the gas phase upon opening the container (pressure release).
- **Molecular Representation**: Red and black space-filling models representing $CO_2$ molecules are overlaid on the liquid. White arrows point upwards, signifying the movement of molecules from the solution into the gas bubbles, demonstrating a shift in the solubility equilibrium:
  $$CO_2(aq) \rightleftharpoons CO_2(g)$$

## Slide 02
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-002

### Text Content
**Chemical Equilibrium**

If the rate at which people move from the first floor to the second equals the rate at which people move from the second floor to the first, the number of people on each floor remains constant and the two populations are in dynamic equilibrium.

### Diagram Analysis: Escalator Analogy
- **Visual Description**: A photograph of two parallel escalators in a public space (likely a mall), one moving upwards and one downwards, with people on both.
- **Pedagogical Significance**: This is a classic macroscopic analogy for **dynamic equilibrium**. Even though individuals are constantly moving (changing position), if the rate of people going up matches the rate of people going down, the total count of people on each floor stays constant. This emphasizes that equilibrium is not "static" but involves balanced, continuous processes.

### Diagram Analysis: Tug-of-War Analogy
- **Visual Description**: A photograph of a tug-of-war competition on a grass field. Two teams are pulling a thick rope in opposite directions.
- **Pedagogical Significance**: This represents a **balance of forces**. In the context of equilibrium, it visualizes the "opposing processes" (forward and reverse reactions) that occur simultaneously. When the forces (rates) are equal and opposite, the system appears stationary at a macroscopic level, even though there is intense activity (tension/molecular collisions) occurring at a microscopic level.

## Slide 04
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-004

### Text Content
**Chemical Equilibrium**
**Evaporation, Condensation and Saturation**

- Initially all liquid
- Gas only, produced
- Balance of gas and liquid production

### Diagram Analysis: Liquid-Vapor Equilibrium
The slide presents three sequential panels showing the establishment of phase equilibrium in a closed container:
1. **Left Panel (Initial State)**: Labeled "No water vapor". Shows pure liquid $H_2O$ with a flat surface. Only the liquid phase is present.
2. **Middle Panel (Approaching Equilibrium)**: Labeled "Unsaturated".
   - A large blue arrow points up (**Evaporation Rate**).
   - A small blue arrow points down (**Condensation Rate**).
   - Net process: More liquid is turning into gas than vice-versa.
3. **Right Panel (Equilibrium State)**: Labeled "Saturated".
   - Two equal-sized blue arrows point in opposite directions (**Evaporation Rate = Condensation Rate**).
   - Pedagogical Note: This state is defined as **saturation**, where the number of molecules leaving the liquid equals the number returning. This is a physical equilibrium.

## Slide 05
> 📷 **[Diagram]** Chemical Equilibrium-005

### Text Content
**Chemical Equilibrium**

**Chemical equilibrium**: A state in which the rates of the forward and reverse reactions are equal and the concentrations of the reactants and products remain constant.

**Equilibrium is a dynamic process** - The stage at which the rate of the forward reaction is equal to the rate of backward reaction is called an equilibrium stage. At this point, the number of reactant molecules converting into products and product molecules into reactants are the same. The same equilibrium can be carried out with the same reactants anywhere with similar conditions with continuous interchanging of molecules hence chemical [equilibrium is dynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_equilibrium).

### Diagram Analysis: Rate and Concentration Graphs
The slide features two critical graphs for the reaction:
$$H_2O (g) + CO (g) \rightleftharpoons H_2 (g) + CO_2 (g)$$

1. **Reaction Rates vs. Time (Left Graph)**:
   - **Forward Rate (Red)**: Starts at a maximum and decreases over time as reactants are consumed.
   - **Reverse Rate (Blue)**: Starts at zero and increases as products are formed.
   - **Equilibrium Point**: The time at which the two curves merge into a single horizontal line, representing $Rate_{forward} = Rate_{reverse}$.

2. **Concentration vs. Time (Right Graph)**:
   - **Reactants ($[CO]$ or $[H_2O]$ - Green)**: Decrease from an initial concentration and plateau.
   - **Products ($[CO_2]$ or $[H_2]$ - Red)**: Increase from zero and plateau.
   - **Equilibrium Region**: Indicated by the dashed line where all concentration curves become horizontal (constant over time).
   - **Note**: The slide mentions that concentration $[X]$ can be replaced by partial pressure $P_X$ for gaseous systems.
