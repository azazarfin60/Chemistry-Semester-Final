## Slide 16

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 16

### Exact Copy of Text
**Elevation of Boiling Point**

* Boiling point is defined as the temperature at which the vapour pressure of liquid becomes equal to the atmospheric pressure.
* Liquids having grater intermolecular forces have high boiling point.
* It is known that a liquid boils only when its vapour pressure becomes equal to the atmospheric pressure. But when a nonvolatile solute is added to the solvent, the vapour pressure is lowered.
* **In order to boil the solution, the temperature has to be raised further so as to make its vapour pressure equal to atmospheric pressure.**
* The boiling point of a solution is higher than the boiling point of the pure solvent.

**Elevation of Boiling Point:** The difference in temperature between the boiling point of a solution and the boiling point of the pure solvent is the boiling-point elevation.

### Diagram Analysis
The slide features a conceptual bar diagram to explain why boiling point increases.
* The left bar represents the pure solvent, where a certain amount of heating is needed to reach the "Boiling" threshold.
* The right bar represents the solution; the starting vapor pressure is "Reduced" due to the solute. Consequently, a much larger "Additional vapor pressure" (requiring higher temperature) must be generated to reach the same atmospheric pressure threshold.
* This visual effectively links the phenomenon of vapor pressure lowering to boiling point elevation.

## Slide 19

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 19

### Exact Copy of Text
**Graphical Re-presentation of Elevation of Boiling Point**

The vapour pressure of the solution containing non-volatile solute is less than that of the pure solvent. Therefore, the solution has to be heated to a higher temperature so that its vapour pressure becomes equal to the atmospheric pressure. Thus the boiling point of the solution is always higher than that of the pure solvent.

The difference in the boiling points of the solution ($T_b$) and pure solvent ($T_b^\circ$) is called the **elevation in boiling point** ($\Delta T_b$).

From The graph-
$BE = T - T^\circ = \Delta T_b$
$BG = P^0 - P$

Hence, vapor pressure of solvent $P^0$ is constant,
$BG \propto \frac{P^0 - P}{P^0}$

From the vapor pressure – temperature of solution curve at different concentration, it can be seen that the curves of solvent and solution located at boiling temperature area are parallel each-other.

Hence,
$\frac{BE}{BG} = \text{Constant}$
$BE \propto BG, \text{But } BE = \Delta T_b$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide provides a more detailed geometric analysis of the P-T graph:
* **Hand-drawn Plot:** It identifies specific points on the solvent (A, B, C) and solution (D, G, E, F) curves.
* **Geometric Relationships:** It defines the vertical drop $BG$ as the lowering of vapor pressure ($P^0 - P$) and the horizontal shift $BE$ as the boiling point elevation ($\Delta T_b$).
* **Parallel Curve Assumption:** It introduces the pedagogical assumption that for dilute solutions, the solvent and solution curves are nearly parallel in the region of the boiling point. This geometric similarity allows for the conclusion that the boiling point elevation is directly proportional to the relative lowering of vapor pressure.

## Slide 20

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 20

### Exact Copy of Text
**Graphical Re-presentation of Elevation of Boiling Point**

$\Delta T_b \propto \frac{P^0 - P}{P^0}$

From the Raoult’s law of lowering of the vapour pressure,

$\frac{P^0 - P}{P^0} = x_2$

or, $\Delta T_b \propto x_2$; or, $\Delta T_b = K^0_b x_2$ [ $K^0_b = \text{Proportionality constant}$ ]

$\Delta T_b = K^0_b \text{ X } \frac{w_2 M_1}{w_1 M_2}$

or, $\Delta T_b = K^0_b \text{ X } \frac{w_2 M_1 \times 1000}{1000 \times w_1 M_2}$

Here, molality, $C_m = \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times w_1}$

Putting the value of $C_m$ in the above equation

$\Delta T_b = K_b \times C_m$ ------------------- (i)

[$K_b = \text{Molal boiling point elevation constant of the solvent}$]

### Diagram Analysis
This slide derives the final equation for boiling point elevation.
* It combines the proportionality found in Slide 19 with Raoult's Law ($x_2$).
* It shows the transition from mole fraction ($x_2$) to molality ($C_m$) by incorporating the mass and molar mass terms.
* The final equation $\Delta T_b = K_b \times C_m$ is the standard form used in chemistry to calculate boiling point changes, with $K_b$ being a characteristic property of the solvent.
