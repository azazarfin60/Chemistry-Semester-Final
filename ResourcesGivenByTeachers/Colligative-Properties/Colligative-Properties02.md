**Raoult’s Law of Lowering of the Vapour Pressure**

Raoult's law : The relative lowering of vapour pressure of a dilute solution is equal to the mole fraction of the solute present in dilute solution.

If the vapour pressure of solvent and solution are $P_o$ and $P$ respectively; the lowering of vapour pressure will be $P_o - P$. Raoult's law can be expressed mathematically,

$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{n}{n + N}$

where ‘n’ = Number of moles of solute and ‘N’ = Number of moles of solvent.

Therefore, $\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = x_2$ ------------------- (i) [ Where, $x_2 = \frac{n}{n + N}$ ]

## Slide 7

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 7

### Exact Copy of Text
**Raoult’s Law of Lowering of the Vapour Pressure**

$1 - \frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = 1 - x_2$

Or, $1 - (1 - \frac{P}{P_o}) = x_1$

Or, $\frac{P}{P_o} = x_1$ [$x_1 = \text{mole fraction of solvent}$]

Or, $P = P_o \times x_1$

Here, vapour pressure of solvent is constant at a given temperature. So, vapour pressure of solution is proportional to the mole fraction of solvent.

**Limitations of Rault’s law**
* Raoult’s law is applicable only to very dilute solutions.
* It is applicable to solutions containing non-volatile solute only.
* It is not applicable to solutes which dissociate or associate in a particular solution.

### Diagram Analysis
This slide completes the mathematical derivation started on Slide 6, showing that the vapor pressure of the solution ($P$) is directly proportional to the mole fraction of the solvent ($x_1$).
It also includes a section in green highlighting the "Limitations of Raoult's law", which are crucial for defining the scope of the theory (ideality, non-volatility, and no particle aggregation/dissociation).

## Slide 8

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 8

### Exact Copy of Text
**Molecular Mass of Solute**
**[In eq. ii--- will be x sign]**

We have, $\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{n}{n + N}$

For very dilute solution, $N \gg n$, the above equation may be written as:

$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{n}{N}$ ------------------- (i)

Let ‘$w_2$’ be the mass of solute, ‘$w_1$’ be the mass of solvent, ‘$M_2$’ the molecular mass of solute and ‘$M_1$’ the molecular mass of solvent. Then, from eq- (i)

$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{w_2/M_2}{w_1/M_1}$

$M_2 = \frac{w_2/M_1}{\frac{-w_1}{P_o - P/P_o}}$ ------------------- (ii)

From eq- (ii), molecular mass of solute can be calculated.

### Diagram Analysis
The slide focuses on the practical application of Raoult's Law: determining the molecular mass of an unknown solute.
It starts with the approximation for "very dilute solutions" where the number of moles of solute ($n$) is negligible compared to the moles of solvent ($N$) in the denominator.
The final expression (ii) rearranges the terms to solve for $M_2$. Note: The visual representation of the final formula in the slide is slightly unconventional due to formatting, but it follows the standard algebraic rearrangement of $n = w_2/M_2$ and $N = w_1/M_1$.

## Slide 9

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 9

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**Example Problem 3:**

An aqueous solution of 2 % nonvolatile solute exerts a pressure of 1.004 bar at the boiling point of the solvent. What is the molar mass of the solute when $P_A^\circ$ is 1.013 bar?

$\frac{\Delta P}{P_A^\circ} = \frac{W_B \times M_A}{M_B \times W_A}$

In a 2 % solution weight of the solute is 2 g and solvent is 98 g

$\Delta P = P_A^\circ - P_{\text{solution}} = 1.013 - 1.004 \text{ bar} = 0.009 \text{ bar}$

$M_B = \frac{P_A^\circ \times W_B \times M_A}{\Delta P \times W_A}$

$M_B = 2 \times 18 \times 1.013 / (98 \times 0.009)$

$= 41.3 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide presents a step-by-step solution to a numerical problem. It demonstrates how to interpret percentage concentration (2% means 2g solute in 100g solution, hence 98g solvent) and apply the relative lowering formula to find the molar mass of the solute ($M_B$). The use of specific units (bar) and chemical constants (molar mass of water = 18) provides a realistic pedagogical example.

**Numerical Problem**

**Question:** The v.p. of water at $20^\circ\text{C}$ is 17.54 mm. When 20 gm. of a nonvolatile solute is dissolved in 100 gm. of water, the v.p. is lowered by 0.30 mm what is the molecular weight of substance?

**Solution:**
p = 17.54 mm.
p - ps = 0.30
w = 20 gm
M of $H_2O$ = 2+16=18
m = ?
W = 100gm.
(p-ps) /p = wM/Wm
0.30/17.54 = 20 x18 /100m
m = 210.48 Ans.
