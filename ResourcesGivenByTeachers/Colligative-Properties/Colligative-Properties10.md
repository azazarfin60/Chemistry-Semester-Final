## Slide 46

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 46

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**3. Human blood has osmotic pressure of 7.2 atm at body temperature of 37°C. Calculate the molar concentration of solute particles in blood. Given R = 0.0821 L atm K$^{-1}$.**
Ans. $\pi = CRT$ ; $C = \frac{\pi}{RT}$ $T = 273 + 37 = 310 \text{ K}$
$C \text{ (molar concentration)} = \frac{7.2}{0.0821 \times 310} = 0.2828 \text{ M}$

**4. Vapour pressure of benzene is 200 mm of Hg. 2g of a non-volatile solute in 78 g benzene has vapour pressure of 195 mm of Hg. Calculate the molar mass of the solute. Molar mass of benzene = 78 g mol$^{-1}$.**
Ans. $\frac{P^o - P}{P^o} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$ ; $\frac{P^o - P}{P^o} = \frac{W_2/M_2}{W_1/M_1}$ ; $\frac{200 - 195}{200} = \frac{2/M_2}{78/78}$
Molar mass of solute $(M_2) = \frac{200 \times 2}{5} = 80 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide presents two practical applications of colligative properties:
1. **Biological Application:** Calculating the molarity of blood particles. This is crucial for medical applications like formulating IV fluids.
2. **Raoult's Law Application:** Using vapor pressure lowering to find the molar mass of a solute in benzene. Note the simplification $n_1 + n_2 \approx n_1$ for dilute solutions is used in the ratio.

## Slide 48

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 48

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**2. Find the osmotic pressure in mm Hg at 15 °C of a solution of naphthalene (C$_{10}$H$_8$) in benzene containing 14 g of naphthalene per litre of solution.**

**Solution:**
We have , $\pi V = nRT$

$\pi \times 1 = \frac{14}{128} \times 0.0821 \times 288$

$\pi = 2.586 \text{ atm} = 2.586 \times 760 = 1965 \text{ mm Hg}$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide provides the worked solution for Problem 2 from the previous slide.
* **Data Point:** Molar mass of naphthalene ($C_{10}H_8$) is $128 \text{ g/mol}$ ($10 \times 12 + 8 \times 1$).
* **Temperature:** $15^\circ\text{C} = 288 \text{ K}$.
* **Pressure Conversion:** The final step converts the result from atmospheres (calculated via $R = 0.0821$) back into "mm Hg" by multiplying by 760.

## Slide 49

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 49

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**5. 300 cm$^3$ of an aqueous solution contains 1.56g of a polymer. The osmotic pressure of such solution at 270°C is found to be 2.57 x 10$^{-3}$ bar. Calculate the molar mass of the polymer**

**Solution:**
Weight of polymer( $W_2$ ) = 1.56g
Osmotic pressure ($\pi$) = 2.57 x 10$^{-3}$ bar
volume (V) = 300 cm$^3$ = 0.3L
$M_2 = W_2 RT / \pi V$
= 1.56 x 0.083 x 300 / 0.3 x 2.57 x 10$^{-3}$
= 50381 g mol$^{-1}$

### Diagram Analysis
This problem focuses on a **Polymer**, which typically has a high molar mass.
* **Unit Adjustment:** $300 \text{ cm}^3$ is converted to $0.3 \text{ L}$.
* **Gas Constant:** $R = 0.083 \text{ L.bar/mol.K}$ is used instead of $0.0821$ because pressure is given in bars.
* **Calculation Note:** Although the text states "270°C", the calculation uses **300** as the temperature value (likely intending $27^\circ\text{C}$ or $300 \text{ K}$). The result **50381 g/mol** is consistent with the use of $T = 300 \text{ K}$.

## Slide 50

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 50

### Exact Copy of Text
**Colligative Properties of Electrolytes**

Because these properties depend on the number of particles dissolved, solutions of electrolytes (which dissociate in solution) show greater changes than those of nonelectrolytes.
e.g. NaCl dissociates to form 2 ion particles; It lowers freezing points almost twice as much as methanol, a nonelectrolyte. $CaCl_2$ dissociates to form 3 ion particles; It lowers freezing points almost three times as much as methanol.

### Diagram Analysis
This slide introduces the **Van't Hoff Factor ($i$)** conceptually, explaining why electrolytes behave differently.
* **Visual Comparison:**
    * The left image shows an ionic crystal (NaCl) being attacked by water molecules and dissociating into individual $Na^+$ and $Cl^-$ ions. This effectively doubles the number of particles.
    * The right image shows methanol molecules dispersed in water but remaining intact as single neutral units.
* **Pedagogical Impact:** It explains the "anomalous" results often seen in experiments where the observed colligative effect is a multiple of the expected theoretical value.
