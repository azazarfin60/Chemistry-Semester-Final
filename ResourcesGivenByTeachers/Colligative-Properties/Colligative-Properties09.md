## Slide 41

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 41

### Exact Copy of Text
**Law of Osmotic Pressure**

1. The osmotic pressure of a solution is proportional to its molarity at constant temperature.
$\pi \propto C$
$\pi = \text{Constant} \times C$ ------------------- (i)
or $\pi \propto 1/V$ [ $C = n/V$; for $n = 1 \text{ mole}$ ]
Then, $\pi V = \text{Constant}$

2. The osmotic pressure of a solution is proportional to the kelven temperature at a given concentration.
$\pi \propto T$
$\pi = \text{Constant} \times T$ ------------------- (ii)

3. Equimolecular quantities of different substances dissolved in the same quantity of a particular solvent raise its osmotic pressure to the same extent at the same temperature.

From Eq (i) and (ii), we get, $\pi = cRT$ [R- molar solution constant]

### Diagram Analysis
This slide breaks down the **Vant-Hoff Equation** into its constituent laws, mirroring gas laws:
* **Law 1 (Analogous to Boyle's Law):** States that at constant temperature, pressure is proportional to concentration (or inversely proportional to volume).
* **Law 2 (Analogous to Charles's Law):** States that at constant concentration, pressure is proportional to absolute temperature (Kelvin).
* **Law 3 (Analogous to Avogadro's Law):** Reinforces the colligative nature, stating that equal numbers of molecules produce equal osmotic pressure regardless of their chemical identity.
* The synthesis leads to the unified equation $\pi = cRT$, where $R$ is the solution constant.

## Slide 42

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 42

### Exact Copy of Text
**Molecular Mass from Osmotic Pressure**

If n mole solute is dissolved in volume V of any solution, then,

$\pi V = nRT = \frac{W}{M} X RT$

or, $M = \frac{W}{\pi V} X RT$

From the above equation, we can determine molecular weight of a solute

### Diagram Analysis
This slide provides the algebraic derivation for calculating the **Molar Mass (M)** of a solute using osmotic pressure data. It substitutes $n = W/M$ into the Vant-Hoff equation. This method is particularly useful for macromolecules like proteins and polymers, where the temperature changes in boiling or freezing points are too small to measure accurately, but osmotic pressure remains significant.

### Exact Copy of Text
**Osmotic Pressure Measurement**

**Barkley and Hartley method**

Fig: Berkeley and Hartley’s pressure apparatus

### Diagram Analysis
This slide illustrates the **Berkeley and Hartley method**, a precision laboratory technique for measuring osmotic pressure:
* **Apparatus Components:** It features a porous porcelain tube containing a copper ferrocyanide [$Cu_2Fe(CN)_6$] semipermeable membrane. The tube is filled with pure solvent.
* **Mechanism:** The tube is surrounded by a metallic jacket containing the solution. Osmosis naturally draws solvent out of the tube.
* **Measurement:** External pressure is applied through a piston until the solvent's meniscus in the capillary tube stops moving (indicating osmosis has been halted). The pressure required, read from the gauge, is exactly equal to the osmotic pressure.
* This method is superior to hydrostatic methods because it prevents the dilution of the solution during measurement.

## Slide 45

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 45

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**Problem:**
Calculate the osmotic pressure of a solution obtained by mixing (a) 100 ml of 3.4 % solution of urea and (b) 100 ml of 1.6 % solution of cane sugar at 20 °C

**Solution:**
We have , $\pi V = nRT$

For osmotic pressure of urea:
$\pi \times \frac{200}{1000} = \frac{3.4}{60} \times 0.0821 \times 293$
$\pi = 6.81 \text{ atm}$

For osmotic pressure of cane sugar:
$\pi \times \frac{200}{1000} = \frac{1.6}{342} \times 0.0821 \times 293$
$\pi = 0.56 \text{ atm}$

Hence, the total osmotic pressure of the mixture: $6.81 + 0.56 = 7.37$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide provides a complex worked example involving the mixing of two different solutions.
* **Key Concept:** It demonstrates that osmotic pressure is additive (Dalton's Law equivalent for solutions).
* **Calculation Nuance:** Note that both urea and cane sugar are calculated using the *final total volume* ($200 \text{ ml} = 0.2 \text{ L}$) because they are now both distributed throughout the entire mixture.
* **Parameters:** $R = 0.0821 \text{ L.atm/mol.K}$, $T = 293 \text{ K}$ (20 + 273).
* The final answer, **7.37 atm**, is the sum of the partial osmotic pressures.
