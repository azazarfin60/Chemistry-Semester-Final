**Numerical Problem**

**Question2 :** The V.P of pure Benzene at certain temp. is 640 mm Hg. A non volatile non electrolyte solid weighing 2.175 gm. is added to 39.0 gm. of Benzene. The V.P. of solution is 600 mm Hg. What is the molecular weight of solid substance?

**Solution:**
p = 640 mm
w= 2.175 gm
W= 39.0 gm
ps=600 mm
m=?
M of benzene = 12x6+1x6
= 72+6=78
(p-ps) /p =wM/Wm
( 640-600)/640 = (2.175 x 78 ) / 39 m
m= 69.6 Ans.

### Diagram Analysis
This slide presents a worked solution for finding the molecular weight of a solute in a benzene solvent. It follows a logical flow: identifying the given data (solvent pressure, solute mass, solvent mass, solution pressure), calculating the solvent's molar mass (benzene, $C_6H_6$), and then using the relative lowering formula to solve for the unknown solute's molecular weight ($m$).

## Slide 13

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 13

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**Problem 1**
The vapour pressure of $CCl_4$ at $30^\circ\text{C}$ is 143 mm of Hg. 0.5 gm of a non-volatile non electrolyte substance with molar mass 65 is dissolved in 100 ml of $CCl_4$. What will be the vapour pressure of the solution. Density of $CCl_4$ at $30^\circ\text{C} = 1.58$ gm per cc.

**Solution**
Vapour pressure of pure solvent, $P^\circ = 143 \text{mm of Hg.}$
Vapour pressure of solution, p = ?
Weight of solute, $W_2 = 0.5 \text{ gm}$
Mol. wt of solute, $M_2 = 65$
Mol. wt of solvent ($CCl_4$), $M_1 = 154$
Weight of solvent, $W_1 = 100 \times 1.58 = 158 \text{ gm}$
(mass = density x volume)

$\frac{P^\circ - p}{P^\circ} = \frac{W_2}{M_2} \cdot \frac{M_1}{W_1}$ (By Raoult's law)

$\frac{143 - p}{143} = \frac{0.5}{65} \times \frac{154}{158}$

$\therefore p = 141.93 \text{ mm of Hg.}$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide addresses a problem where the solvent's mass must be calculated from its volume and density. It provides the molar mass of carbon tetrachloride ($CCl_4$) as 154 g/mol. The solution correctly applies Raoult's Law to find the resulting vapor pressure of the solution ($p$), showing how a small amount of solute causes a measurable drop in pressure.

## Slide 14

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 14

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**Example – 02:**

The vapour pressure of a solution containing $13 \times 10^{-3} \text{ kg}$ of solute in 0.1 kg of water at 298 K is 27.371 mm Hg. calculate the molar mass of the solute. Given that the vapour pressure of water at 298 K is 28.065 mm Hg.

**Given:** mass of solute $W_2 = 13 \times 10^{-3} \text{ kg}$, mass of solvent (water) $= W_1 = 0.1 \text{ kg}$, vapour pressure of pure solvent (water) $= p^\circ = 28.065 \text{ mm of Hg}$, vapour pressure of solution $= p = 27.371 \text{ mm of Hg}$, temperature $= T = 298 \text{ K}$, Molecular mass of solvent (water) $= M_1 = 18 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$

**To Find:** Molecular mass of solute $= M_2 = ?$

**Solution:**
For dilute solution relative lowering of vapour pressure is given by
$\frac{p^\circ - p}{p^\circ} = \frac{W_2 M_1}{W_1 M_2}$
$\therefore M_2 = \frac{W_2 M_1}{W_1} \times \frac{p^\circ}{p^\circ - p}$
$\therefore M_2 = \frac{13 \times 10^{-3} \text{kg} \times 18 \text{g}}{0.1 \text{kg}} \times \frac{28.065 \text{ mm of Hg}}{28.065 \text{ mm of Hg} - 27.371 \text{ mm of Hg}}$
$\therefore M_2 = \frac{13 \times 10^{-3} \times 18 \text{g}}{0.1} \times \frac{28.065 \text{ mm of Hg}}{0.694 \text{ mm of Hg}} = 94.63 \text{ g}$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide demonstrates a molecular mass calculation using SI units (kg) for mass. It follows a rigorous format of stating "Given", "To Find", and "Solution". It highlights the precision required in such calculations, as seen in the small difference in vapor pressure ($0.694 \text{ mm Hg}$) used in the denominator.

## Slide 15

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 15

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

Calculate the vapor pressure of a solution made by dissolving 50.0 g $CaCl_2$, $C_6H_{12}O_6$, in 500 g of water. The vapor pressure of pure water is 47.1 torr at $37^\circ\text{C}$

**Solution**
To use Raoult's Law (Equation 1), we need to calculate the mole fraction of water (the solvent) in this salt-water solution.
$\chi_{\text{solvent}} = \frac{\text{moles of water}}{\text{moles of solute} + \text{moles of solvent}}$
$\chi_{\text{solvent}} = \frac{n_{\text{water}}}{n_{\text{solutes}} + n_{\text{water}}}$

The molar mass of $CaCl_2$ is 111 g/mol and of water is 18 g/mol. So
$n_{\text{water}} = \frac{500 \text{ g}}{18 \text{ g/mol}} = 27.7 \text{ mol}$
$n_{\text{solutes}} = \frac{50 \text{ g}}{111 \text{ g/mol}} = 0.45 \text{ mol}$

but this is really:
* $n_{Ca^{2+}} = 0.45 \text{ mol}$
* $n_{Cl^-} = 0.9 \text{ mol}$

and
$\chi_{\text{solvent}} = \frac{27.7 \text{ mol}}{0.45 \text{ mol} + 0.9 \text{ mol} + 27.7 \text{ mol}} = 0.953$

Note that this still relatively dilute. The pressure of the solution is then calculated via Raoult's Law (Equation 1):
$P_{\text{solution}} = 0.953 \times 47.1 = 44.88 \text{ torr}$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide is particularly important as it introduces the effect of **electrolytic dissociation** on colligative properties.
* Although 50.0g of $CaCl_2$ corresponds to 0.45 moles of the formula unit, the solution contains 0.45 moles of $Ca^{2+}$ and 0.9 moles of $Cl^-$ (totaling 1.35 moles of ions).
* The calculation explicitly accounts for this by adding both ion counts to the denominator of the mole fraction equation.
* This demonstrates why electrolyte solutes have a more significant impact on vapor pressure lowering than non-electrolytes of the same molar concentration.
* Note: The chemical formula $C_6H_{12}O_6$ appears in the question text but seems to be a typo or artifact, as the solution focuses entirely on $CaCl_2$.
