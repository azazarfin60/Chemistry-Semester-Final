![Slide 2](file:///home/azaz/Downloads/Documents/Colligative-Properties/Colligative-Properties-002.png)

### Exact Copy of Text
**Colligative Properties**

A colligative property may be defined as one which depends on the number of particles in solution and not in any way on the size or chemical nature of the particles.

Dilute solution containing non volatile solutes exhibit the following properties
* Lowering of vapor pressure
* Elevation of boiling point
* Depression of freezing point
* Osmotic pressure

The word "colligative" comes from the Latin word *colligatus*, which means "bound together", referring to how the properties of a solvent are bound to the concentration of solute in a solution. These properties are related to one another. Thus, if one is measured, the other can be calculated. The results are excellent if the following three conditions are satisfied.
* The solution should be very dilute
* The solute should be non-volatile
* The solute does not dissociate or associate in solution

**Vapor Pressure**
* As solute molecules are added to a solvent, the solvent become less volatile (=decreased vapor pressure).
* Solute-solvent interactions contribute to this effect.
* Therefore, the vapor pressure of a solution is lower than that of the pure solvent.

### Diagram Analysis
This slide is text-heavy and structured with bullet points. It provides the formal definition of colligative properties, emphasizing that they depend solely on the *number* of particles. It lists the four main colligative properties and provides etymological context for the term "colligative". It also outlines the necessary conditions for these properties to be observed (dilute, non-volatile, no dissociation/association).

### Diagram Analysis
The slide uses multiple pedagogical illustrations to explain the microscopic basis of vapor pressure lowering:
1. **Particle Replacement Model:** Shows two containers. The "Pure Solvent" surface is entirely blue particles. The "Solution" surface has some blue particles replaced by red solute particles, reducing the area available for solvent evaporation.
2. **Molecular Comparison:** Two beakers show water molecules (red and white spheres). In the "WATER + SUGAR" beaker, large orange sugar molecules occupy surface positions, physically blocking water molecules from escaping into the gas phase ($P_{WS} < P_W$).
3. **Ionic Solution View:** Compares pure water to a 1.0 M NaCl solution. It highlights that ionic solutes dissociate into multiple particles (Na+ in blue and Cl- in green), which are then solvated by water molecules. This illustrates why ionic solutes have a larger effect on colligative properties per mole of formula unit.

### Diagram Analysis
This slide presents a quantitative representation of vapor pressure lowering:
* **Graph:** A linear plot shows Vapor Pressure on the y-axis against "Water %" on the x-axis. As the percentage of water (solvent) increases from 0% to 100%, the vapor pressure increases linearly, reaching the maximum value $P_w$ for pure water.
* **Table:** Complements the graph by listing specific compositions. For instance, at 20% water, the vapor pressure is $P_{ws1}$, which is significantly lower than $P_w$.
* The label "Ideal condition" implies that this linear relationship (Raoult's Law) holds perfectly for ideal solutions where solute-solvent interactions are similar to solvent-solvent interactions.

## Slide 5

![Slide 5](file:///home/azaz/Downloads/Documents/Colligative-Properties/Colligative-Properties-005.png)

### Exact Copy of Text
**Lowering of Vapor Pressure**

On dissolving the non-volatile solute in a pure solvent, the vapor pressure of a pure solvent gets gradually decreased. Let us assume that p is the vapor pressure of the solvent and $p_s$ is the vapor pressure of the solution, then the lowering of vapor pressure can be written as $(p - p_s)$. This lowering of vapor pressure relative to the vapor pressure of the pure solvent is called the Relative lowering of Vapour pressure. Thus,

Relative Lowering of Vapour Pressure = $\frac{p - p_s}{p}$

(a) **Pure solvent**
Solvent particle
Higher vapor pressure

(b) **Solution containing nonvolatile solute**
Solute particle
Lower vapor pressure

### Diagram Analysis
The slide introduces the mathematical derivation of "Relative Lowering of Vapor Pressure".
* **Formula:** It defines $p$ as solvent vapor pressure and $p_s$ as solution vapor pressure. The relative lowering is given by the ratio of the change in pressure to the initial pressure: $(p - p_s) / p$.
* **Comparison Diagrams:**
    * **(a) Pure Solvent:** A beaker of blue liquid shows many solvent particles (blue spheres) in the headspace, indicating high vapor pressure.
    * **(b) Solution:** A beaker containing a non-volatile solute (green spheres) shows fewer solvent particles (blue spheres) in the headspace, visually representing the "Lower vapor pressure". Solute particles are shown at the surface, obstructing evaporation.
