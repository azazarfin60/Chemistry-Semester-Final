## Slide 21

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 21

### Exact Copy of Text
**Raoult’s Law of Elevation of the Boiling Point**

1. The boiling point elevation of a solution is proportional to its molality
$\Delta T_b \propto C_m$
or, $\Delta T_b = K_b \times C_m$
[$K_b$ = Proportionality constant known as molal boiling point elevation constant of the solvent]

2. Equimolecular quantities of different substances dissolved in the same quantity of a particular solvent raise its boiling point to the same extent.

**Table 9.3 Molal boiling point elevation constant ‘$K_b$’ for some solvents**

| S. No. | Solvent | $T_b^\circ$ (K) | $K_b$ (K kg mol$^{-1}$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1. | Water | 373.15 | 0.52 |
| 2. | Ethanol | 351.5 | 1.20 |
| 3. | Benzene | 353.3 | 2.53 |
| 4. | Chloroform | 334.4 | 3.63 |
| 5. | Ether | 307.8 | 2.02 |
| 6. | Carbon tetrachloride | 350.0 | 5.03 |
| 7. | Carbon disulphide | 319.4 | 2.42 |
| 8. | Acetic acid | 391.1 | 2.93 |
| 9. | Cyclohexane | 353.74 | 2.79 |

**Molal Boiling-point Elevation Constant:**
It is defined as the elevation in the boiling point of a solvent when one mole of a non-volatile solute is dissolved in one kilogram of solvent. It is also known as the ebullioscopic constant. The unit of molal elevation constant is given as K kg mol$^{-1}$.

### Diagram Analysis
This slide summarizes the key properties of the molal boiling point elevation constant ($K_b$):
* **Proportionality:** It reinforces that $\Delta T_b$ depends on molality, not the identity of the solute (as long as it's non-volatile).
* **Reference Data:** Table 9.3 provides a comprehensive list of $K_b$ values and boiling points for common solvents, ranging from water (0.52) to carbon tetrachloride (5.03). This data is essential for solving numerical problems.
* **Formal Definition:** The bottom section provides the formal definition and units for the "ebullioscopic constant", which is the standardized measure of a solvent's sensitivity to added solutes.

## Slide 22

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 22

### Exact Copy of Text
**Molecular Mass from Elevation of Boiling Point**

From the Raoult’s law, we have
$\Delta T_b \propto C_m$
or, $\Delta T_b = K_b \times C_m$ ------------------- (i)

Here, molality, $C_m = \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times w_1}$

Putting the value of $C_m$ in the equation- (i), we get,

$\Delta T_b = K_b \times \frac{w_2 \times 1000}{M_2 \times w_1}$

[$K_b$ = Molal boiling point elevation constant of the solvent]

### Diagram Analysis
This slide provides the algebraic substitution needed to use boiling point elevation for experimental molecular weight determination. It expands the molality term ($C_m$) into its component variables: mass of solute ($w_2$), mass of solvent ($w_1$), and molar mass of solute ($M_2$). This form of the equation is the most directly applicable to laboratory data.

## Slide 23

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 23

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**Problem:**
0.75 g of an unknown substance is dissolved in 200 g water. If the elevation of boiling point is 0.15 K and molal elevation constant is $7.5 \text{ K Kg mol}^{-1}$ then, calculate the molar mass of unknown substance

$\Delta T_b = K_b \text{ m}$
$= K_b \times W_2 \times 1000 / M_2 \times W_1$

$M_2 = K_b \times W_2 \times 1000 / \Delta T_b \times W_1$

$= 7.5 \times 0.75 \times 1000 / 0.15 \times 200$

$= 187.5 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide presents a worked example for calculating the molar mass of an unknown substance.
* It rearranges the boiling point elevation formula to solve for $M_2$.
* The steps show clear variable substitution: $K_b = 7.5$, $W_2 = 0.75\text{g}$, $\Delta T_b = 0.15\text{K}$, and $W_1 = 200\text{g}$.
* The calculation uses the "1000" factor to convert the solvent mass from grams to kilograms within the formula.

## Slide 25

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Slide 25

### Exact Copy of Text
**Numerical Problem**

**Problem**
A solution containing 2.5 g of a non-volatile solute in 100 gm of benzene boiled at a temperature 0.42K higher than at the pure solvent boiled. What is the molecular weight of the solute? The molal elevation constant of benzene is $2.67 \text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$.

$K_b = 2.67 \text{ K kg mol}^{-1}$
$\Delta T_b = 0.42 \text{ K}$
$W_1 = 100 \text{ g} = \frac{100}{1000} \text{ kg} = 0.1 \text{ Kg}$
$W_2 = 2.5 \text{ g}$

$M_2 = \frac{K_b}{\Delta T_b} \cdot \frac{W_2}{W_1}$

$M_2 = \frac{2.67}{0.42} \times \frac{2.5}{0.1}$

$M_2 = 158.98 \text{ g mol}^{-1}$

### Diagram Analysis
This slide provides another worked example, this time using benzene as the solvent.
* It demonstrates an alternative way to handle units: converting the solvent mass ($W_1$) to kilograms (0.1 Kg) before plugging it into the formula, rather than using the "1000" factor within the formula.
* The final result, $158.98 \text{ g/mol}$, is calculated with high precision, reflecting the typical accuracy expected in ebullioscopic determinations.
