# Chemical Kinetics: Slides 016-020

## Slide 16

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Factors Affecting Rate: Concentration and Temperature

### **Factors Affecting the Rate**

#### **Concentration**
* Reacting molecules must **Collide** with proper **Orientation** & sufficient **Energy**
* Above factors increases as conc. increases & hence **Rate** increases

**Diagram Analysis (Concentration):**
* **Left Image:** Shows a container with a few molecules (red and green spheres). The text notes: "Here we have a few molecules. There are few collisions. The rate of reaction is low."
* **Right Image:** Shows the same container but with a much higher density of molecules. The text notes: "Here we have many molecules. There are more collisions. The rate of reaction is greater."
* **Concept:** Increasing concentration increases the frequency of effective collisions.

#### **Temperature**
* As **T** increases avg **K.E** increases
* As avg. **K.E** increases, the particles move faster & **Collision energy** & **Collision frequency** increases & hence **Rate** increases

**Diagram Analysis (Temperature):**
The graph plots **Reaction Rate $k$ ($s^{-1}$)** against **Temp (K)**:
* It shows an exponential curve, illustrating that even small increases in temperature can lead to a significant increase in the reaction rate constant.

## Slide 17

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Effect of Catalyst on Activation Energy

### **Factors Affecting the Rate**

#### **Catalyst**
* A substance that increases the reaction rate without undergoing a chemical change itself
* Provides an alternate reaction mechanism faster than mechanism in absence of catalyst
* Lowers the activation energy for a chemical reaction

**Diagram Analysis:**
The energy profile diagram compares the **uncatalyzed** (red) and **catalyzed** (blue) pathways:
* **Reactants to Products:** Both paths start and end at the same energy levels.
* **Activation Energy ($E_a$):** The uncatalyzed path has a high peak, requiring significant energy. The catalyzed path has a much lower peak, indicating a lower activation energy barrier.
* **Result:** Because the barrier is lower, more molecules have sufficient energy to react at a given temperature, increasing the rate.

## Slide 18

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Reaction Half-Life Visualization

### **Reaction Half-Life**

**Diagram Analysis:**
This graph illustrates the concept of **half-life ($t_{1/2}$)** for a first-order reaction:
* **Y-axis:** Concentration of A.
* **X-axis:** Time (arbitrary units).
* **Progression:**
    * At $t = 0$, the concentration is $[A]_0$ (represented by 16 spheres).
    * After one half-life ($t_{1/2}$), the concentration drops to $[A]_0/2$ (8 spheres).
    * After two half-lives ($2t_{1/2}$), it drops to $[A]_0/4$ (4 spheres).
    * After three half-lives ($3t_{1/2}$), it drops to $[A]_0/8$ (2 spheres).
* **Key Concept:** For a first-order reaction, the time taken for the concentration to halve is constant, regardless of the initial concentration.

## Slide 19

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Pseudo-Order Reaction and Example

### **Pseudo-Order Reaction**

The reaction that appears to be an $n^{th}$ order reaction but belongs to some different order is called Pseudo order reaction.

If concentration of a reactant remains constant (because it is a catalyst or because it is in excess with respect to other reactants), its concentration can be included in rate constant thus making(causing) the reaction to be pseudo first order or sometimes pseudo second order reaction.

**Example: Hydrolysis of Cane Sugar –**

$$C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} + H_2O \rightarrow C_6H_{12}O_6 + C_6H_{12}O_6$$
$$\text{Sucrose} + \text{Water} \rightarrow \text{Glucose} + \text{Fructose}$$

$$\text{Rate of reaction} = k [C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}] [H_2O]$$

But $[H_2O] \gg [C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}]$

So, concentration of water can be approximated as constant as its concentration doesn't change a lot during the reaction.

Now rate of reaction can be written as –
$$r = k' [C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}]$$
where $k' = k [H_2O]$

Thus, hydrolysis of cane sugar is **pseudo first order reaction**.

## Slide 20

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Zero-Order Reaction Kinetics

### **Zero-Order Reaction**

When the rate of reaction is independent of the concentration of the reactants or in other words the rate of reaction is dependent on the zero power of the reactants, this order is called zero order reactions.

The rate limiting factors for such kind of reactions are not the concentration, but the solubility, pH, temperature etc.

So if we prepare a graph between rate of reaction and time, then we will get straight line, because every moment of time, the rate of reaction is same or constant, no slope is there.

And as the reaction goes in a constant way, so the concentration of the reactants will get reduce also in a constant manner. i.e.

$$[A] = [A_0] - kt$$

* $A$ = Concentration of reactant at any time $t$
* $A_0$ = Initial Concentration of reactant at any time 0

**Diagram Analysis:**
* **Top Graph (Rate vs. Time):** Shows a horizontal line at height $k$ with **Slope = 0**, confirming the rate is constant over time.
* **Bottom Graph (Concentration vs. Time):** Shows a linear decrease in concentration from $[A_0]$, where the slope of the line is $-k$.
