# Atomic Structure: Slides 096-100

## Slide 96

### **Octet Rule**

The octet rule states that the elements with eight electrons in the valence shell tend to be more stable and so, elements form bonds with other elements in such a way that they achieve this state.

> 📷 **[Diagram]** CCl4 Lewis Structure

**Diagram Analysis:**
The slide illustrates the octet rule using **Carbon tetrachloride ($CCl_4$)**:
- **Lewis Dot Structure (Left):** Shows Carbon as the central atom sharing four pairs of electrons with four Chlorine atoms. Each atom (C and all four Cl) is surrounded by 8 dots, representing a full octet.
- **Bond Structure (Right):** Shows the four single covalent bonds represented by lines, with the remaining six non-bonding electrons (lone pairs) around each Chlorine atom.

## Slide 97

### **Limitations of Octet Rule**

1. **Incomplete Octet:** Beryllium has two, and Boron, or Aluminium, has three valence electrons. In compounds like $BeCl_2$ or $BCl_3$, the central Boron atom does not complete its octet yet form covalent bonds, which the octet rule could not explain.

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Incomplete Octet Examples

**Diagram Analysis:**
- **$BeCl_2$ Formation:** $Be$ (2 electrons) + $2Cl$ (7 electrons each) $\to$ $Cl-Be-Cl$.
    - **Result:** Beryllium gets four electrons and **does not** attains a octet configuration. Chlorine attains an octet.
- **$BCl_3$ Formation:** $B$ (3 electrons) + $3Cl$ (7 electrons each) $\to$ $BCl_3$.
    - **Result:** Boron gets six electrons and **does not** attains a octet configuration. Chlorine attains an octet.

## Slide 98

### **Limitations of Octet Rule**

2. **Expanded Octet:** Elements of the 3rd period and above have d-orbitals that take part in bond formation reactions. This allows the atom to incorporate a higher number of orbitals, leading to such atoms forming a greater number of bonds.

Phosphorus can form five bonds as **$PF_5$**, and Sulphur can form six bonds as **$SF_6$**, thereby expanding the octet to ten and twelve.

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Expanded Octet Examples

**Diagram Analysis:**
- **$PF_5$:** Phosphorus (central atom) is bonded to five Fluorine atoms, resulting in **10 valence electrons** around Phosphorus.
- **$SF_6$:** Sulfur (central atom) is bonded to six Fluorine atoms, resulting in **12 valence electrons** around Sulfur.

## Slide 99

### **Limitations of Octet Rule**

2. **Odd Electron:** The octet rule does not satisfy all the atoms of nitric oxide (**·NO**) and Nitrogen dioxide (**·NO$_2$**) which have an odd number of electrons.

The odd electron- containing atoms are reactive species, unstable, and some may largely exist as dimers.

> 📷 **[Diagram]** Odd Electron Species

**Diagram Analysis:**
- **Nitric Oxide ($NO$):** The Nitrogen atom has 7 electrons in its valence environment (a lone pair, a double bond to Oxygen, and one **free radical** electron).
- **Nitrogen Dioxide ($NO_2$):** The Nitrogen atom has an unpaired electron (free radical) while being bonded to two Oxygen atoms.
- **Key:**
    - **Blue dot:** free radical
    - **Red dots:** lone pair

## Slide 100

### **Limitations of Octet Rule**

3. The elements whose valence shell has a maximum of only 2 electrons, like that of Hydrogen(H) or Helium(He) are stable without following the octet rule. For them, the **duplet rule** is valid.

4. Transition and inner transition elements do not follow this rule. There are plenty of stable transition elements with more than 8 electrons in their valence shell.

**Applications of Octet Rule**
- This rule explains the formation of covalent and ionic bonds.
- Explains the high reactivity of elements close to noble gases.
- Explains the high stability and inertness of the noble gases.
- Helps in predicting the properties of p-block elements.
