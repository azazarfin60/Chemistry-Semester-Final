## Slide 026

### Reference Electrodes

The slide illustrates three types of standard reference electrodes used in electrochemistry:

#### 1. The Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)
*   **Components:** A glass hood through which $H_2$ gas at 1 atm pressure is bubbled. Inside is a platinum (Pt) electrode covered with platinum black (covered with $H_2$). It is immersed in a 1 M solution of $H^+$ ions at 25°C.
*   **Notation:** $Pt, H_2 (1 \text{ atm}) \mid H^+ \parallel$

#### 2. Calomel Electrode
*   **Components:** A glass tube containing liquid mercury ($Hg$) at the top, followed by a layer of calomel ($Hg_2Cl_2$ paste). A platinum wire provides electrical contact. It is separated from a saturated $KCl$ solution by a porous plug. A sintered glass disc is at the bottom for ionic contact.
*   **Notation:** $Hg(l) \mid Hg_2Cl_2 (s), KCl (\text{aq, saturated}) \parallel$

#### 3. Glass Electrode
*   **Components:** An silver ($Ag$) wire coated with silver chloride ($AgCl$) immersed in 1 M $HCl$. This is contained within a thin-walled glass bulb sensitive to hydrogen ions.
*   **Notation:** $Ag, AgCl \mid 1 \text{ M } HCl \vdots H^+ \text{ (Test Solution)}$

## Slide 027

### Classification of Electrochemical Cell

1.  **Concentration Cell**
    a)  **Electrolyte Concentration Cell:** Consists of two identical electrodes dipped in solutions of the same electrolyte but at different concentrations (e.g., $Cu$ in 0.0100 M $Cu^{2+}$ and $Cu$ in 0.100 M $Cu^{2+}$).
    b)  **Electrode concentration cell (Gas Electrode):** Two gas electrodes with the same gas but at different pressures (e.g., $H_2$ at $P_1$ and $P_2$) dipped in the same solution.
    c)  **Amalgam Cell:** Uses metal amalgams of different concentrations as electrodes.

#### Diagram Analysis:
*   **Electrolyte Concentration Cell (Top Right):** Shows two $Cu$ electrodes in different $[Cu^{2+}_{aq}]$ concentrations, connected by a $KNO_3$ salt bridge and a voltmeter.
*   **Gas Electrode Concentration Cell (Bottom Right):** Shows two $H_2$ gas inlets at pressures $P_1$ and $P_2$ into $HCl(a)$ solutions, connected by a U-shaped bridge.
*   **Amalgam Cell (Bottom Left):** Illustrates an H-shaped Weston cell. The left limb contains $Cd(Hg)$ amalgam at the base with $CdSO_4$ crystals. The right limb contains liquid $Hg$, $Hg_2SO_4$ paste, and $CdSO_4$ crystals. Both limbs are topped with saturated $CdSO_4$ solution.

### 2. Chemical Cell

Chemical cells are classified based on the presence or absence of liquid junction potential:

#### Without transference
In this setup, the two half-cells are physically separated, and the ionic connection is made via a salt bridge.
*   **Diagram:** Shows two separate beakers (each with a $Zn$ electrode in $ZnSO_4$ of different concentrations $c_1$ and $c_2$) connected by a salt bridge with a porous plug. A VTVM (Vacuum Tube Voltmeter) measures the potential.
*   **Notation:** Electrolyte concentration cell without transference.

#### With transference
In this setup, the two electrolytic solutions are in direct contact, creating a liquid junction.
*   **Diagram:** Shows a single container where solutions $AX_n$ and $BX_m$ meet at a boundary (dashed line). Electrodes $A$ and $B$ are immersed. $\Phi'$ and $\Phi''$ indicate the potential at different points.
*   **Notation:** Scheme of a chemical cell with transference.

## Slide 029

### Cell potential or emf

The flow of current through the circuit is determined by the ‘push’ of electrons at the anode and ‘attraction’ of electrons at the cathode. These two forces constitute the ‘driving force’ or ‘electrical pressure’ that sends electrons through the circuit. This driving force is called the electromotive force (abbreviated **emf**) or cell potential. The emf or cell potential is measured in units of volts (V) and is also referred to as cell voltage.

#### Standard emf of a cell
The emf generated by an electrochemical cell is given by the symbol $E$. It can be measured with the help of a potentiometer. The value of emf varies with the concentration of the reactants and products in the cell solutions and the temperature of the cell. When the emf of a cell is determined under standard conditions, it is called **the standard emf.**

The standard conditions are:
(a) 1 M solutions of reactants and products; and
(b) temperature of 25°C.

**The emf of a cell with 1 M solutions of reactants and products in solution measured at 25°C.**

Standard emf of a cell is represented by the symbol **E°**. With gases, 1 atm pressure is a standard condition instead of concentration.

For a simple Zn-Cu voltaic cell, the standard emf, E°, is 1.10 V. This means that the emf of the cell operated with $[Cu^{2+}]$ and $[Zn^{2+}]$ both at 1 M and 25°C is 1.10 V.
$$Zn \mid Zn^{2+} (aq, 1M) \parallel Cu^{2+} (aq, 1M) \mid Cu \quad E^\circ = + 1.1 V$$

## Slide 030

### Determination of emf of a half-cell

By a single electrode potential, we also mean the emf of an isolated half-cell or its half-reaction. The emf of a cell that is made of two half-cells can be determined by connecting them to a voltmeter. However, there is no way of measuring the emf of a single half-cell directly.

A convenient procedure to do so is to combine the given half-cell with another standard half-cell. The emf of the newly constructed cell, $E$, is determined with a voltmeter. The emf of the unknown half-cell, $E^\circ$ can then be calculated from the expression:

$$E_{\text{measured}} = E_R - E_L$$

*   If the standard half-cell acts as anode, the equation becomes:
    $$E_R = E_{\text{measured}} \quad (\because E_L = 0)$$
*   On the other hand, if standard half-cell is cathode, the equation takes the form:
    $$E_L = - E_{\text{measured}} \quad (\because E_R = 0)$$
