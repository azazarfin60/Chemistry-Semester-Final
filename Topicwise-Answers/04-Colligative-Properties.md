### Q.6(a) What is osmosis? Describe the laws of osmotic pressure. (04)

**Answer:**

**Osmosis:**
Osmosis is the spontaneous, net movement of solvent molecules (e.g., water) through a semi-permeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration (a dilute solution or pure solvent) into a region of higher solute concentration (a more concentrated solution). This flow aims to equalize the concentrations on both sides of the membrane.

**Laws of Osmotic Pressure (van 't Hoff's Laws):**
The osmotic pressure ($\pi$ or $P$) of a dilute solution behaves similarly to the pressure of an ideal gas. J.H. van 't Hoff established two empirical laws governing it:
1.  **Boyle-van 't Hoff Law (Concentration Law):** At a constant temperature, the osmotic pressure ($\pi$) of a dilute solution is directly proportional to its molar concentration ($C$).
    $$\pi \propto C \quad \text{(at constant } T)$$
    Since $C = n/V$, this can be written as $\pi \propto 1/V$ (the osmotic pressure is inversely proportional to the volume containing a given mass of solute).
2.  **Charles-van 't Hoff Law (Temperature Law):** For a solution of a given concentration, the osmotic pressure ($\pi$) is directly proportional to the absolute temperature ($T$ in Kelvin).
    $$\pi \propto T \quad \text{(at constant } C)$$

**Combined Equation:**
Combining both laws, we get: $\pi \propto C \cdot T$.
Introducing a proportionality constant $R$ (which is numerically equal to the ideal gas constant):
$$\pi = CRT$$
Where $\pi$ = osmotic pressure, $C$ = molarity (moles/Liter), $R$ = universal gas constant, $T$ = absolute temperature.

### Q.6(b) The vapour pressure of an aqueous solution of glucose is lower than that of water. Analyze the reason. (03)

**Answer:**

**Reasoning based on Raoult's Law (Lowering of Vapor Pressure):**
When a non-volatile solute, such as solid glucose, is dissolved in a volatile solvent like water, the resulting aqueous solution exhibits a vapor pressure lower than that of the pure water at the same temperature.

1.  **Surface Area Blockage:** Vaporization is a surface phenomenon. In pure water, the entire surface is occupied by volatile water molecules that can readily escape into the vapor phase.
2.  **Reduced Escape Rate:** In an aqueous glucose solution, the surface is occupied by both volatile water molecules and non-volatile glucose molecules. Since glucose does not vaporize, the fraction of the surface area available for water molecules to escape is reduced. 
3.  **Equilibrium Shift:** Consequently, the rate of evaporation of water decreases. To re-establish liquid-vapor equilibrium, the rate of condensation must also decrease, which means fewer water vapor molecules are present in the gas phase above the liquid. 
4.  **Result:** Fewer vapor molecules exert less pressure. Therefore, the vapor pressure of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent.

### Q.6(c) Calculate the osmotic pressure of a 5% solution of glucose at $18^\circ C$. (03)

**Answer:**

**Given Data:**
*   Concentration = 5% (w/v) solution of glucose. This means 5 grams of glucose ($C_6H_{12}O_6$) are dissolved in 100 mL of solution.
*   Mass of solute ($W$) = 5 g
*   Volume of solution ($V$) = $100 \text{ mL} = 0.1 \text{ Liters}$
*   Temperature ($T$) = $18^\circ C = 18 + 273.15 = 291.15 \text{ K}$
*   Molar mass of glucose ($M$) = $(12 \times 6) + (1 \times 12) + (16 \times 6) = 180 \text{ g/mol}$
*   Gas constant ($R$) = $0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}$

**To Find:**
*   Osmotic pressure ($\pi$) in atmospheres.

**Solution:**
**Step 1: Calculate Molar Concentration ($C$)**
Moles of glucose ($n$) = $\frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Molar Mass}} = \frac{5}{180} = 0.02778 \text{ moles}$
Concentration ($C$) = $\frac{n}{V} = \frac{0.02778 \text{ mol}}{0.1 \text{ L}} = 0.2778 \text{ mol/L}$ (or Molarity, M)

**Step 2: Apply van 't Hoff equation**
$$\pi = CRT$$
$$\pi = (0.2778 \text{ mol/L}) \times (0.0821 \text{ L atm K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}) \times (291.15 \text{ K})$$
$$\pi = 0.2778 \times 23.903$$
$$\pi = 6.64 \text{ atm}$$

**Final Answer:** The osmotic pressure of the 5% glucose solution at $18^\circ C$ is **$6.64 \text{ atmospheres}$**.

### Q.3(a) What is osmosis? Discuss the Berkeley and Hartley's method for the determination of osmotic pressure. (05)

**Answer:**

**Osmosis:**
Osmosis is the spontaneous, net movement of solvent molecules through a semi-permeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration (pure solvent) into a region of higher solute concentration.

**Berkeley and Hartley's Method:**
This is a highly precise laboratory technique for measuring osmotic pressure. It is based on applying external pressure to the solution just sufficient to stop the osmotic flow of solvent into it.

**Apparatus and Procedure:**
1.  **Structure:** The apparatus consists of an inner porous porcelain tube. Within the walls of this tube, a semipermeable membrane of copper ferrocyanide [$Cu_2Fe(CN)_6$] is chemically deposited.
2.  **Setup:** The porous tube is fitted with a solvent reservoir (capillary tube) on one end to monitor the solvent level. The tube is completely filled with pure solvent (e.g., water).
3.  **Outer Jacket:** The porous tube is concentrically surrounded by a strong metallic jacket (gun-metal) that contains the solution whose osmotic pressure is to be measured. The outer jacket is connected to a pressure gauge and a piston mechanism.
4.  **Mechanism:** Naturally, osmosis begins, and solvent starts flowing from the inner porous tube across the membrane into the outer solution jacket. This causes the meniscus level in the capillary indicator tube to fall.
5.  **Measurement:** External hydrostatic pressure is applied to the solution via the piston. The pressure is carefully adjusted until the solvent meniscus in the capillary tube stops moving (i.e., it is pushed back to its original mark).
6.  **Result:** At this exact point, the applied external pressure has perfectly counterbalanced the osmotic driving force. The reading on the pressure gauge is exactly equal to the **osmotic pressure ($\pi$)** of the solution.

**Advantages:** It is rapid and accurate because it prevents the dilution of the solution during measurement (since no net solvent transfer is allowed to occur).

### Q.3(b) Define: Reverse osmosis and Isotonic solution. (04)

**Answer:**

**Reverse Osmosis:**
If a pressure greater than the osmotic pressure is applied to the solution side of a semi-permeable membrane, the natural direction of osmosis is reversed. Pure solvent is forced out of the concentrated solution and into the pure solvent side. This phenomenon is called reverse osmosis. It is widely used industrially for the desalination of seawater and purification of drinking water.

**Isotonic Solution:**
Two solutions are said to be isotonic if they exert exactly the same osmotic pressure when separated by a semi-permeable membrane at a given temperature. Because their osmotic pressures are equal ($\pi_1 = \pi_2$), there is no net flow of solvent across the membrane between them. (If $\pi_1 = \pi_2$, then $C_1 = C_2$ at the same temperature, meaning they are equimolar).

### Q.2(a) State and explain the laws of osmotic pressure. (04)

**Answer:**

**Laws of Osmotic Pressure (van 't Hoff's Laws):**
The osmotic pressure ($\pi$ or $P$) of a dilute solution behaves similarly to the pressure of an ideal gas. J.H. van 't Hoff established two empirical laws governing it:

1.  **Boyle-van 't Hoff Law (Concentration Law):** At a constant temperature, the osmotic pressure ($\pi$) of a dilute solution is directly proportional to its molar concentration ($C$).
    $$\pi \propto C \quad \text{(at constant } T)$$
    Since $C = n/V$, this can be written as $\pi \propto 1/V$ (the osmotic pressure is inversely proportional to the volume containing a given mass of solute).
2.  **Charles-van 't Hoff Law (Temperature Law):** For a solution of a given concentration, the osmotic pressure ($\pi$) is directly proportional to the absolute temperature ($T$ in Kelvin).
    $$\pi \propto T \quad \text{(at constant } C)$$

**Explanation (Combined Equation):**
Combining both laws, we get: $\pi \propto C \cdot T$.
Introducing a proportionality constant $R$ (which is numerically equal to the ideal gas constant):
$$\pi = CRT$$
Where $\pi$ = osmotic pressure, $C$ = molarity (moles/Liter), $R$ = universal gas constant, $T$ = absolute temperature. This equation shows that equal volumes of isotonic solutions at the same temperature contain an equal number of solute molecules (Avogadro-van 't Hoff Law).

### Q.2(b) How is molecular weight of a solute determined from osmotic pressure? (04)

**Answer:**

**Determination of Molecular Weight:**
The molar mass (molecular weight) of an unknown solute can be calculated directly using the van 't Hoff equation for osmotic pressure. This method is particularly useful for macromolecules like proteins and polymers.

According to the van 't Hoff equation:
$$\pi V = nRT$$

Where:
*   $\pi$ = Osmotic pressure
*   $V$ = Volume of the solution
*   $n$ = Number of moles of the solute
*   $R$ = Universal gas constant
*   $T$ = Absolute temperature

We know that the number of moles ($n$) is equal to the mass of the solute ($W$) divided by its molar mass ($M$):
$$n = \frac{W}{M}$$

Substituting the value of $n$ into the osmotic pressure equation:
$$\pi V = \left( \frac{W}{M} \right) RT$$

Rearranging the equation to solve for the molar mass ($M$):
$$M = \frac{W \cdot R \cdot T}{\pi \cdot V}$$

By experimentally measuring the osmotic pressure ($\pi$) of a solution containing a known mass ($W$) of solute in a known volume ($V$) at a specific temperature ($T$), the exact molecular weight ($M$) of the solute can be determined using this formula.

### Q.5(a) What is lowering of vapor pressure? State and explain Raoult's law of lowering of vapor pressure. (06)

**Answer:**

**Lowering of Vapor Pressure:**
When a non-volatile solute (e.g., sugar or salt) is dissolved in a pure volatile solvent (e.g., water), it is experimentally observed that the vapor pressure of the resulting solution is lower than the vapor pressure of the pure solvent at the same temperature. 
If $P_o$ is the vapor pressure of the pure solvent and $P$ is the vapor pressure of the solution, then the difference $(P_o - P)$ is known as the **lowering of vapor pressure**. This occurs because solute molecules occupy surface sites, reducing the number of solvent molecules that can escape into the vapor phase.

**Raoult's Law of Lowering of Vapor Pressure:**
*   **Statement:** *"The relative lowering of vapor pressure of a dilute solution is equal to the mole fraction of the solute present in the dilute solution."*
*   **Explanation:** The "relative lowering" is the ratio of the lowering of vapor pressure to the vapor pressure of the pure solvent: $\frac{P_o - P}{P_o}$.
    Mathematically, Raoult's law is expressed as:
    $$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = x_2$$
    Where $x_2$ is the mole fraction of the solute.
    Since $x_2 = \frac{n}{n + N}$ (where $n$ = number of moles of solute, $N$ = number of moles of solvent), we can write:
    $$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{n}{n + N}$$

### Q.5(b) How can you determine the molecular weight of a solute from lowering of vapor pressure? (03)

**Answer:**

From Raoult's Law, we have the exact relationship:
$$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{n}{n + N}$$

For **very dilute solutions**, the number of moles of solute ($n$) is negligible compared to the number of moles of solvent ($N$). Therefore, in the denominator, $(n + N)$ can be approximated as $N$.
The equation simplifies to:
$$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{n}{N}$$

Now, let:
*   $w_2$ = mass of the solute
*   $M_2$ = molecular weight of the solute (so $n = w_2 / M_2$)
*   $w_1$ = mass of the solvent
*   $M_1$ = molecular weight of the solvent (so $N = w_1 / M_1$)

Substituting these into the simplified equation:
$$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{w_2 / M_2}{w_1 / M_1}$$
$$\frac{P_o - P}{P_o} = \frac{w_2 \cdot M_1}{w_1 \cdot M_2}$$

Rearranging to solve for the molecular weight of the solute ($M_2$):
$$M_2 = \frac{w_2 \cdot M_1 \cdot P_o}{w_1(P_o - P)}$$
By experimentally measuring the pure solvent vapor pressure ($P_o$) and the solution vapor pressure ($P$) for known masses ($w_1, w_2$), $M_2$ can be easily calculated.

### Q.5(c) Mention the limitations of Raoult's law of lowering of vapor pressure. (03)

**Answer:**

**Limitations of Raoult's Law:**
1.  **Dilution Requirement:** It is strictly applicable only to **very dilute solutions** (usually concentration less than 5%). Concentrated solutions show significant deviations due to strong solute-solvent interactions.
2.  **Non-Volatile Solute:** It applies only to solutions containing a perfectly **non-volatile solute**. If the solute is volatile, it will contribute to the total vapor pressure, complicating the simple relationship.
3.  **No Association/Dissociation:** It is not applicable to solutes that either **dissociate** into ions (like electrolytes: NaCl, HCl) or **associate** into larger molecules (like acetic acid in benzene) within the solvent. These processes change the actual number of solute particles in the solution, altering the mole fraction unpredictably.

### Q.7(a) Define the terms: Osmosis, Isotonic Solution and Reverse Osmosis. (06)

**Answer:**

1.  **Osmosis:** The spontaneous, net movement of solvent molecules through a semi-permeable membrane from a region of lower solute concentration (or pure solvent) into a region of higher solute concentration, driven by the desire to equalize concentrations on both sides.
2.  **Isotonic Solution:** Two different solutions are said to be isotonic if they exert exactly the same osmotic pressure when separated by a semi-permeable membrane at a given temperature. Because their osmotic pressures are equal ($\pi_1 = \pi_2$), there is zero net flow of solvent across the membrane between them.
3.  **Reverse Osmosis:** A process where a mechanical pressure greater than the natural osmotic pressure is applied to the concentrated solution side of a semi-permeable membrane. This overcomes the natural osmotic drive and forces pure solvent molecules to move *out* of the concentrated solution and into the pure solvent side (reversing the natural flow). It is used for water purification and desalination.

### Q.7(b) State and explain laws of osmotic pressure. (03)

**Answer:**

The osmotic pressure ($\pi$) of a dilute solution obeys laws analogous to the ideal gas laws, formulated by van 't Hoff:

1.  **Boyle-van 't Hoff Law:** At a constant temperature, the osmotic pressure ($\pi$) of a dilute solution is directly proportional to its molar concentration ($C$).
    $$\pi \propto C \quad \text{(at constant } T)$$
    Since $C = n/V$, it implies $\pi \propto 1/V$.
2.  **Charles-van 't Hoff Law:** At a given constant concentration, the osmotic pressure ($\pi$) of a dilute solution is directly proportional to the absolute temperature ($T$ in Kelvin).
    $$\pi \propto T \quad \text{(at constant } C)$$

Combining these yields the **van 't Hoff equation for solutions**: $\pi = CRT$ (where $R$ is the solution constant, equal to the universal gas constant).

### (b) Osmotic pressure of dilute solution
Osmotic pressure ($\pi$) is a fundamental colligative property of a dilute solution. It is defined as the exact amount of external mechanical pressure that must be applied to the solution side of a semi-permeable membrane to precisely halt the natural inward osmotic flow of pure solvent.
According to the van 't Hoff equation for dilute solutions, **$\pi = CRT$** (where $C$ is molar concentration, $R$ is the gas constant, $T$ is absolute temperature).
*Significance:* Osmotic pressure is uniquely valuable among colligative properties because its magnitude is relatively large even for extremely dilute solutions. Therefore, it is the primary and most accurate method used to experimentally determine the enormous molecular weights of macromolecules, polymers, and biological proteins that cannot withstand boiling or freezing.
