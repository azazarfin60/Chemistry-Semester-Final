## Class Test 01: Solutions and Colligative Properties

### Q.1 Define the term solution. Molality is independent of temperature while molarity is a function of temperature- Give the reason. (5)
**Answer:**
**Solution:** A solution is defined as a perfectly homogeneous physical mixture of two or more chemically non-reacting substances, the composition of which can be varied within certain limits.
**Reason for Temperature Dependence:**
*   **Molality ($m$)** is defined as the number of moles of solute dissolved per kilogram ($1000 \text{ g}$) of the **solvent's mass**. Because the physical mass of a substance does not expand or contract when heated or cooled, molality is completely independent of temperature changes.
*   **Molarity ($M$)** is defined as the number of moles of solute dissolved per liter ($1 \text{ dm}^3$) of the **solution's volume**. Unlike mass, the physical volume of a liquid expands upon heating and contracts upon cooling. Therefore, as temperature changes, the volume changes, causing the molarity to change accordingly.

### Q.2 State Henry's law. Write its limitations. (4)
**Answer:**
**Henry's Law:** At a constant temperature, the mass of a gas dissolved in a given volume of a liquid solvent is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas present in equilibrium with the liquid. ($m = kP$)
**Limitations:**
The law is only strictly applicable under the following ideal conditions:
1.  The pressure of the gas is not too high, and the temperature is not too low.
2.  The solution is dilute.
3.  The gas does not undergo any chemical reaction with the solvent (e.g., $NH_3$ in water reacts to form $NH_4OH$, violating the law).
4.  The gas does not undergo dissociation or association in the solution.

### Q.3 Distinguish between Ideal solution and Non-ideal solution. (4)
**Answer:**

| Feature | Ideal Solution | Non-Ideal Solution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Raoult's Law** | Obeys Raoult's law perfectly at all concentrations and all temperatures. | Does not obey Raoult's law; shows positive or negative deviations. |
| **Enthalpy Change** | $\Delta H_{\text{mix}} = 0$ (No heat is absorbed or evolved when mixing). | $\Delta H_{\text{mix}} \neq 0$ (Heat is either evolved or absorbed). |
| **Volume Change** | $\Delta V_{\text{mix}} = 0$ (Total volume is exactly the sum of individual volumes). | $\Delta V_{\text{mix}} \neq 0$ (Volume expands or contracts upon mixing). |
| **Intermolecular Forces** | Solute-Solvent (A-B) attractive forces are perfectly equal to Solute-Solute (A-A) and Solvent-Solvent (B-B) forces. | Solute-Solvent (A-B) forces are either stronger or weaker than the pure component forces. |

### Q.4 Discuss briefly the effect of temperature on solubility of solid in liquid. (4)
**Answer:**
The effect of temperature on the solubility of a solid solute in a liquid solvent depends entirely on the enthalpy of solution ($\Delta H_{\text{sol}}$), governed by Le Chatelier's Principle:
1.  **Endothermic Dissolution ($\Delta H > 0$):** If the solid absorbs heat when dissolving (e.g., $KNO_3$, $NH_4Cl$, $KCl$), raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium forward to absorb the added heat. Thus, **solubility increases with an increase in temperature**.
2.  **Exothermic Dissolution ($\Delta H < 0$):** If the solid releases heat when dissolving (e.g., $Li_2SO_4$, $NaOH$, $Ca(OH)_2$), raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium backward to get rid of the excess heat. Thus, **solubility decreases with an increase in temperature**.

### Q.5 Calculate the molality of a solution prepared by dissolving 10 g of the antifreeze ethylene glycol ($C_2H_6O_2$) in 450 g of water. (3)
**Answer:**
**1. Calculate Molar Mass of Solute:**
Molar mass of Ethylene glycol ($C_2H_6O_2$) = $(2 \times 12) + (6 \times 1) + (2 \times 16) = 24 + 6 + 32 = 62 \text{ g/mol}$.
**2. Calculate Moles of Solute:**
Moles ($n$) = $\text{Given Mass} / \text{Molar Mass} = 10 \text{ g} / 62 \text{ g/mol} = 0.1613 \text{ moles}$.
**3. Calculate Mass of Solvent in kg:**
Mass of water = $450 \text{ g} = 0.45 \text{ kg}$.
**4. Calculate Molality ($m$):**
Molality = $\text{Moles of solute} / \text{Mass of solvent in kg}$
$m = 0.1613 \text{ moles} / 0.45 \text{ kg} = \mathbf{0.358 \text{ mol/kg (or 0.358 m)}}$.

## Class Test 02: Chemical Bonding

### 1. Explain different types of van-der-Waals attraction forces using proper figure.
**Answer:**
Van der Waals forces are weak, short-range intermolecular attractive forces that exist between neutral molecules. They are classified into three main types based on the polarity of the interacting molecules:

1.  **Dipole-Dipole Interactions (Keesom Forces):**
    These occur between two permanently polar molecules (e.g., $HCl$). The partially positive end ($\delta^+$) of one permanent dipole is electrostatically attracted to the partially negative end ($\delta^-$) of a neighboring permanent dipole.
    *(Mental Figure: Two oval molecules, each with a $\delta^+$ on the left and $\delta^-$ on the right, aligning end-to-end so $\delta^-$ attracts $\delta^+$).*
2.  **Dipole-Induced Dipole Interactions (Debye Forces):**
    These occur when a permanent polar molecule approaches a completely non-polar molecule (e.g., $H_2O$ and $O_2$). The strong electric field of the permanent dipole distorts the symmetrical electron cloud of the non-polar molecule, "inducing" a temporary dipole within it. The two molecules then attract each other.
    *(Mental Figure: A permanent polar molecule distorting a perfectly spherical non-polar electron cloud into a temporary oval shape, causing attraction).*
3.  **Instantaneous Dipole-Induced Dipole Interactions (London Dispersion Forces):**
    These are the only forces that exist between completely non-polar molecules or noble gas atoms (e.g., $He, N_2, CH_4$). Due to the constant, random motion of electrons, the electron cloud can briefly shift to one side, creating an "instantaneous, temporary dipole." This fleeting dipole instantly distorts the electron cloud of an adjacent molecule, creating an "induced dipole," and they attract. These are the weakest forces but exist in all molecules.
    *(Mental Figure: A symmetrical atom suddenly having more electrons on one side, creating a temporary $\delta^-$ pole, which pushes away electrons on a neighboring atom, inducing a matching $\delta^+$ pole).*

### 2. "$N_2$ is diamagnetic but $O_2$ is paramagnetic" justify this statement with M.O.T.
**Answer:**
According to Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT), a molecule is diamagnetic if all of its electrons are paired in molecular orbitals, and paramagnetic if it contains one or more unpaired electrons.

**1. Nitrogen Molecule ($N_2$):**
Total electrons = $7 + 7 = 14$ electrons.
M.O. Configuration: $\sigma 1s^2, \sigma^* 1s^2, \sigma 2s^2, \sigma^* 2s^2, (\pi 2p_x^2 = \pi 2p_y^2), \sigma 2p_z^2$
*Justification:* Every single molecular orbital is filled with exactly two electrons (spin-paired). Because there are absolutely **zero unpaired electrons**, the $N_2$ molecule is repelled by a magnetic field, making it completely **diamagnetic**.

**2. Oxygen Molecule ($O_2$):**
Total electrons = $8 + 8 = 16$ electrons.
M.O. Configuration: $\sigma 1s^2, \sigma^* 1s^2, \sigma 2s^2, \sigma^* 2s^2, \sigma 2p_z^2, (\pi 2p_x^2 = \pi 2p_y^2), (\pi^* 2p_x^1 = \pi^* 2p_y^1)$
*Justification:* According to Hund's rule, the two highest energy electrons must singly occupy the two degenerate anti-bonding pi orbitals ($\pi^* 2p_x$ and $\pi^* 2p_y$) with parallel spins before pairing. Because the $O_2$ molecule possesses exactly **two unpaired electrons**, it is attracted to an external magnetic field, proving it is strongly **paramagnetic**.

## Class Test 03: Chemical Kinetics

### Q.1 Define rate of reaction. Discuss briefly how rate of reaction depends on temperature and reactant concentration. (7)
**Answer:**
**Rate of Reaction:** It is defined as the change in the molar concentration of any one of the reactants or products per unit of time. (Rate = $\pm \frac{dC}{dt}$).

**Dependence on Temperature:**
The rate of almost all reactions increases exponentially with an increase in temperature. According to the **Collision Theory**, increasing the temperature shifts the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution curve of molecular energies, massively increasing the specific fraction of molecules that possess kinetic energy equal to or greater than the Activation Energy ($E_a$). With vastly more molecules capable of "effective collisions," the rate surges. (Governed by the Arrhenius equation: $k = A e^{-E_a/RT}$).

**Dependence on Reactant Concentration:**
The rate of a reaction is generally directly proportional to the concentration of the reactants. According to the **Law of Mass Action**, a higher concentration means there are more reactant molecules crowded into a given volume. This crowding drastically increases the frequency of random collisions between molecules. More total collisions lead to a proportionally higher number of effective collisions per unit time, thereby increasing the overall reaction rate.

### Q.3 Derive an expression for the rate constant of a first order reaction. (5)
**Answer:**
Consider a first-order reaction: $A \rightarrow \text{Products}$.
Let the initial concentration of $A$ at time $t = 0$ be '$a$' mol/L.
After time $t$, let '$x$' mol/L of $A$ react. The remaining concentration is $(a - x)$.
The rate of reaction is directly proportional to the first power of the remaining concentration:
$$\frac{dx}{dt} \propto (a - x)^1$$
$$\frac{dx}{dt} = k(a - x)$$
Rearranging variables:
$$\frac{dx}{a - x} = k dt$$
Integrating both sides:
$$\int \frac{dx}{a - x} = \int k dt$$
$$-\ln(a - x) = kt + C \quad \dots \text{(Equation 1)}$$
Apply initial conditions to find $C$: At $t = 0$, $x = 0$.
$$-\ln(a - 0) = k(0) + C \implies C = -\ln a$$
Substitute $C$ back into Equation 1:
$$-\ln(a - x) = kt - \ln a$$
$$kt = \ln a - \ln(a - x) = \ln \left( \frac{a}{a - x} \right)$$
Converting natural log ($\ln$) to base-10 log ($\log$):
$$kt = 2.303 \log \left( \frac{a}{a - x} \right)$$
**$$k = \frac{2.303}{t} \log \left( \frac{a}{a - x} \right)$$**
This is the required integrated rate equation for a 1st order reaction.

### Q.4 Show that the time required to complete a given fraction of reaction is independent of initial concentration of reactant. (3)
**Answer:**
Using the first-order integrated rate equation derived above:
$$t = \frac{1}{k} \ln \left( \frac{a}{a - x} \right)$$
Let the "given fraction" of the reaction completed be denoted by '$f$'. Therefore, the amount reacted '$x$' is equal to that fraction of the initial concentration: $x = f \cdot a$.
Substitute this into the equation:
$$t = \frac{1}{k} \ln \left( \frac{a}{a - fa} \right)$$
Factor out '$a$' in the denominator:
$$t = \frac{1}{k} \ln \left( \frac{a}{a(1 - f)} \right)$$
The initial concentration '$a$' cancels out completely:
**$$t = \frac{1}{k} \ln \left( \frac{1}{1 - f} \right)$$**
Because the rate constant ($k$) is a constant at a given temperature, and the fraction ($f$) is a chosen constant value, the calculated time ($t$) depends purely on constants. This proves mathematically that the time required to complete any specific fraction of a first-order reaction is completely independent of the starting concentration '$a$'.

## Class Test 04: Electrochemistry

### Q.1 Define molar conductance and equivalent conductance. Apply Debye-Huckel theory to calculate the activity coefficients of $Na^+$ and $SO_4^{2-}$ ions of 0.01 molal $Na_2SO_4$ solution in water at room temperature. (10)
**Answer:**
*   **Molar Conductance ($\Lambda_m$):** It is defined as the total conductance of all the ions produced by dissolving exactly one mole of an electrolyte in a volume ($V$) of solution. ($\Lambda_m = \kappa \times \frac{1000}{M}$, where $M$ is molarity).
*   **Equivalent Conductance ($\Lambda_{eq}$):** It is defined as the total conductance of all the ions produced by dissolving exactly one gram-equivalent weight of an electrolyte in a volume ($V$) of solution. ($\Lambda_{eq} = \kappa \times \frac{1000}{N}$, where $N$ is normality).

**Debye-Hückel Calculation:**
*(Note: This is the exact same calculation requested in the 2017 Exam, Section B, Q6(c). The results are summarized below).*
1.  **Calculate Ionic Strength ($I$):**
    For 0.01m $Na_2SO_4$, $I = \frac{1}{2} [ (0.02)(+1)^2 + (0.01)(-2)^2 ] = 0.03$.
2.  **Calculate Activity Coefficients ($\gamma$) using $\log \gamma_i = -0.509 z_i^2 \sqrt{I}$:**
    *   **For $Na^+$ ion:** $\log \gamma_+ = -0.509(1)^2\sqrt{0.03} = -0.08816 \implies \mathbf{\gamma_+ = 0.816}$
    *   **For $SO_4^{2-}$ ion:** $\log \gamma_- = -0.509(-2)^2\sqrt{0.03} = -0.3526 \implies \mathbf{\gamma_- = 0.444}$

### Q.2 State Kohlrausch's law. Derive the equation to find out the $k_w$ of water by using the law. (10)
**Answer:**
**Kohlrausch’s Law of Independent Migration of Ions:**
It states that at infinite dilution, where dissociation is 100% complete and inter-ionic electrostatic forces are exactly zero, each individual ion migrates independently and makes a definite, fixed contribution to the total equivalent conductance of the electrolyte, regardless of the nature of the other ion it is associated with.
Mathematically: $\Lambda^\circ_{\text{eq}} = \lambda^\circ_+ + \lambda^\circ_-$

**Derivation of Ionic Product of Water ($K_w$):**
Water is an extremely weak electrolyte that ionizes slightly:
$$H_2O \rightleftharpoons H^+ + OH^-$$
Let the degree of dissociation of water be $\alpha$. According to Arrhenius theory, the degree of dissociation is the ratio of molar conductance at a given concentration ($\Lambda_v$) to the molar conductance at infinite dilution ($\Lambda^\circ$):
$$\alpha = \frac{\Lambda_v}{\Lambda^\circ}$$
1.  **Finding $\Lambda^\circ$:** By applying Kohlrausch's law, the molar conductance of water at infinite dilution is the sum of its ionic mobilities (which are known constants from strong electrolytes):
    $$\Lambda^\circ_{H_2O} = \lambda^\circ_{H^+} + \lambda^\circ_{OH^-}$$
2.  **Finding $\Lambda_v$:** The actual molar conductance of pure water can be found experimentally using its highly sensitive specific conductance ($\kappa$). If $C$ is the molar concentration of pure water ($1000 \text{ g/L} / 18 \text{ g/mol} = 55.55 \text{ mol/L}$), then:
    $$\Lambda_v = \kappa \times \frac{1000}{C}$$
3.  **Substituting into $\alpha$:**
    $$\alpha = \frac{\kappa \times \frac{1000}{C}}{\lambda^\circ_{H^+} + \lambda^\circ_{OH^-}} = \frac{1000 \cdot \kappa}{C (\lambda^\circ_{H^+} + \lambda^\circ_{OH^-})}$$
4.  **Calculating $K_w$:** The ionic product of water is defined as $K_w = [H^+][OH^-]$.
    From the ionization equation, $[H^+] = C\alpha$ and $[OH^-] = C\alpha$.
    $$K_w = (C\alpha) \times (C\alpha) = C^2 \alpha^2$$
    Substitute the full expression for $\alpha$ into the $K_w$ equation:
    $$K_w = C^2 \left[ \frac{1000 \cdot \kappa}{C (\lambda^\circ_{H^+} + \lambda^\circ_{OH^-})} \right]^2$$
    $$K_w = C^2 \frac{(1000 \cdot \kappa)^2}{C^2 (\lambda^\circ_{H^+} + \lambda^\circ_{OH^-})^2}$$
    The $C^2$ terms perfectly cancel out, yielding the final equation:
    **$$K_w = \left[ \frac{1000 \cdot \kappa}{\lambda^\circ_{H^+} + \lambda^\circ_{OH^-}} \right]^2$$**
This derived equation elegantly allows the determination of the fundamental constant $K_w$ by simply measuring the specific conductance ($\kappa$) of ultra-pure water.
